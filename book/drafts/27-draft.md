# 27. FULL AND FINAL SETTLEMENT, 1913-1958

![FULL AND FINAL SETTLEMENT, 1913-1958](../images/image7.jpg)


*Westminster, Chungking, London, Dublin, Peking, Port Said, and Rome*

7 August 1913. Edwin Montagu, Under-Secretary of State for India, moved the East India Revenue Accounts in the House of Commons. The year just closed had run a surplus of nearly £8,000,000 over estimate, "due mainly to the very large railway receipts and the high prices obtained for opium." The year ahead was different. "This is the first Budget," Montagu said, "in which no receipts can be expected from the Indo-Chinese opium traffic." The opium estimate was £306,000, £4,250,000 less than the year before. He still forecast a surplus of nearly £1,500,000, without any increase of taxation. The Calcutta chest had become a revenue line India could lose.

The colonies kept a smaller version of the trade. On 13 February 1929 Leopold Amery answered for the Colonial Office on Hong Kong: "The Colonial Government has established a legal Government monopoly of the sale of opium with the intention of restricting consumption by charging high prices for Government opium." Smuggled opium from China, he said, kept the monopoly from working.

Whitehall decided not to restart it. 10 November 1943, a written answer from Colonel Oliver Stanley, Secretary of State for the Colonies, to Captain Gammans. Total prohibition of opium smoking in British territories in the Far East "which are now in enemy occupation." The prepared opium monopolies "will not be re-established on their re-occupation." Hong Kong was one of those territories. On 23 August 1945 the Prime Minister told the Commons that the Japanese surrender in the colony would be accepted by a British Force Commander.

Ten months before that answer: 11 January 1943, 重庆 Chóngqìng, Chungking on the paper. Sir Horace Seymour signed for the United Kingdom, Hugh Richardson for India, 宋子文 Sòng Zǐwén, Tse Vung Soong, for the Republic of China. Article 2 abrogated every treaty provision that let His Majesty exercise jurisdiction over British subjects in China. Article 4 returned the International Settlements at Shanghai and Amoy to Chinese administration, and with them "the British Concession at Canton." HMSO printed it as Cmd. 6456 at one shilling and threepence. The treaty does not mention Hong Kong.

Ireland had settled five years earlier. 25 April 1938, London, three agreements under one preamble, signed by Éamon de Valera, Seán Lemass, Seán MacEntee, and Séamas Ó Riain for Éire, and Neville Chamberlain, Sir John Simon, Sir Samuel Hoare, and Malcolm MacDonald for the United Kingdom. Éire would pay £10,000,000 sterling on or before 30 November 1938. The payment "shall constitute a final settlement of all financial claims of either of the two Governments against the other arising out of matters occurring before the date of this Agreement."

Two days later de Valera moved approval in the Dáil and read out what the money settled. A British despatch of 9 April 1932 had quoted the 1926 heads of settlement: the Free State would pay "the full amount of the annuities accruing due from time to time under the Irish Land Acts, 1891 to 1909, without any deduction whatsoever." The Wyndham Act of 1903 sits inside that span, and the Leinster sale with it. London valued the land annuities claim at a capital sum of £78,000,000, de Valera told the House, with £20,000,000 more in pensions and local loans. Britain had been collecting £5,000,000 a year, in his round figure, through special duties on Irish goods. The 1938 settlement priced the Irish land debt the way the land acts had priced Irish land, in years of income. "We have agreed to this payment, which represents two years' purchase of the sum which the British were collecting by means of these special duties," he said. "You may, if you like, regard it as ransom money."

Article 3 listed what the settlement left open. The £250,000 a year for damage to property under the agreement of 3 December 1925. Unredeemed bank notes. Trustee savings banks. Double taxation. And one line on its own: "Withdrawal of United Kingdom silver coin from Éire." Silver had been the metal Canton took for tea in 1839. In 1938 it was British coin in Irish pockets, and paying for its withdrawal was left to a separate arrangement.

Irish farmers went on paying the annuity after 1938, to the Irish Land Commission, and the money stayed in Dublin. Erskine Childers, Minister for Lands, gave the Dáil the figures on 24 April 1957. Annuities had been halved in 1933 and stayed low. Arrears at 31 March 1957 came to £142,562, "approximately a quarter of 1 per cent. of the total amount collectable since 1933." Of 111,000 tenanted holdings vested in the Land Commission since 1923, about 13,000 still waited to pass to the tenants. Between those two dates the state had left the Crown's dominions. The Ireland Act 1949 recorded that Éire "ceased, as from the eighteenth day of April, nineteen hundred and forty-nine, to be part of His Majesty's dominions," and in the next section declared that the Republic of Ireland "is not a foreign country" for any law in force in the United Kingdom.

1 October 1949. 北京 Běijīng. 毛泽东 Máo Zédōng read out the proclamation of the Central People's Government. The next morning's 人民日报 Rénmín Rìbào carried the line 毛泽东主席宣读中央人民政府公告 in its front-page headline: Chairman Mao Zedong reads the proclamation. The text named Beijing the capital and 周恩来 Zhōu Ēnlái premier and foreign minister. It called the new government 唯一合法政府 wéiyī héfǎ zhèngfǔ, the sole legal government, and offered diplomatic relations to any foreign government that would observe 平等、互利及互相尊重领土主权 píngděng, hùlì jí hùxiāng zūnzhòng lǐngtǔ zhǔquán: equality, mutual benefit, and mutual respect for territory and sovereignty. London recognised that government on 6 January 1950. A British chargé d'affaires reached Peking on 13 February. On 17 June he asked whether the Chinese wished to exchange ambassadors. In February 1951 the Foreign Office told the Commons: "No reply has been received."

The canal account stayed open longest. In June 1893 the Commons was told that the shares bought in 1875 for £4,000,000 were worth an estimated £17,750,000, and that 75 per cent of the tonnage through the canal was British. In 1932 the dividends paid to the Treasury came to £2,440,251. On 2 August 1956 Julian Amery told the Commons that "six weeks ago the last British soldier was withdrawn from the Canal Zone."

The Egyptian law that nationalised the company was No. 285 of 1956, dated 26 July. Anthony Eden's answer the next morning was a statement of breach: "The unilateral decision of the Egyptian Government to expropriate the Suez Canal Company, without notice and in breach of the Concession Agreements." His first measures, reported on 30 July, were paper. An order under the Exchange Control Act put Egypt "out of the transferable account area." A direction under the Defence (Finance) Regulations safeguarded "the securities and gold of the Suez Canal Company." On 2 August he gave the figures: nearly 70 million tons of oil through the canal the year before, about half the oil supplies of Western Europe; compensation to the shareholders at about £70 million; the canal's net annual revenue at about £10 million.

On 6 November Eden told the House that British and French troops had landed at Port Said and that his forces would cease fire at midnight. Technicians were to start "the clearing of the obstructions in the Suez Canal" at once. On 3 December Selwyn Lloyd announced that the withdrawal from the Port Said area "can now be carried out without delay."

The money was settled in Rome. Heads of Agreement, 29 April 1958, signed for the United Arab Republic by Abdel Galeel El Emary and for the Suez stockholders by J. Georges-Picot and two others. "As a full and final settlement of the compensation due to shareholders," the United Arab Republic would pay the equivalent of £E28.3 million and leave the company its assets outside Egypt. The first £E5.3 million was already in hand: transit tolls that shipowners had paid to the company in London and Paris after 26 July 1956. The balance came in six annual instalments, the last £E3 million due on 1 January 1964, not less than 40 per cent of each in sterling.

On 22 July 1958 Gerald Nabarro asked the Chancellor what the Treasury held. Derick Heathcoat Amory answered: "Her Majesty's Government own 353,804 shares in the Suez Finance Company. Their current market value is about £16 million. No decision has been taken about the future of the Government's holding."
