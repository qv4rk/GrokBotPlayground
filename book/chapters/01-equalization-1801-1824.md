# 1. EQUALIZATION, 1801–1824

![EQUALIZATION, 1801–1824](../images/image13.jpg)

*The Liberties, Dublin*

A hand loom is a wooden frame roughly the size of a bed. The warp — every thread that will run the length of the finished cloth — winds onto a beam at the back and draws forward through heddles, cord or wire eyes that lift some threads and leave others down. The weaver's feet work treadles below; press one and a gap opens in the warp. Through that gap goes the shuttle, a hollow piece of wood carrying a bobbin of weft, thrown right hand to left hand, caught, and beaten forward against the last thread. Treadle, throw, catch, beat. An inch of cloth for an hour of steady hands.

Dublin's version of the trade had a name for its finest output: poplin, a silk warp woven against a wool weft. The silk gave the surface its sheen; the wool gave the cloth enough weight to hold a fold and take a dye. The people who made it lived where they worked, in the Liberties — the streets west of Dublin Castle along the Coombe, Cork Street, Pimlico, Weaver's Square — a house doubling as workshop, beds in the back room, children winding bobbins, a wife or journeyman at a second frame. Huguenot refugees had carried the silk half of the trade in with them after fleeing France a century earlier. The Weavers' Guild of Dublin had been meeting on the Coombe since 1446.

In 1745 the guild built a hall on the strength of that trade: fifty-six feet by twenty-one, wainscoted, portraits of kings on the walls, money from the Huguenot banker David Digges La Touche. Five years later they set a statue of George II into the brick above the door, a weaver's shuttle carved into his hand, and recorded the gesture in their own minutes as sincere loyalty. Three hundred years already stood behind the guild, and every sign in the hall pointed to more ahead.

Then, on 1 January 1801, Article VI of the Act of Union came into force: a ten-percent reciprocal duty on manufactured goods moving between Britain and Ireland, for twenty years exactly. The expiry sat inside the clause itself — 1 January 1821. Anyone in that hall who could read a statute could read that far.

Two hundred miles east, on the Irwell at Manchester, a water wheel — later a steam engine — ran a shaft the length of a mill floor, leather belts dropping off it onto dozens of power looms at once. Cartwright had patented the design in 1785; by the 1810s it filled rooms across Manchester, Stockport, Bolton, running through the night, since a steam engine keeps no hours a daylight weaver would recognize. Calico, fustian, and plain cottons came off those floors at prices no hand-loom district could match, once the Irish duty stopped standing in the way.

War with France had already cut off the raw silk the Liberties needed. In 1798 enough of the district's weavers went out with the United Irishmen that London kept the Coombe on file as a population worth watching, and Emmet's rising in 1803 put a second mark on the same page. That year, Combination Laws already in force in Britain since 1800 were extended to Ireland on a harder scale — six months in gaol or three months hard labour for organizing, against three months or two months hard labour in England. Pooling money toward a powered loom, agreeing a common price, meeting as a union: all of it fell under that statute and stayed a crime until 1824.

Rain still ruined cloth left out on tenters. In 1815 the philanthropist Thomas Pleasants financed the Tenter House on Cork Street, three storeys of brick with a stove inside, so the stretching and drying could go on through bad weather. Contemporary accounts describe men caught in earlier downpours as "exposed to great distress, and not unfrequently reduced either to the hospital or the gaol." The stove kept a few more bolts of cloth coming off Dublin beams every winter; the tariff schedule outside it kept ticking down regardless.

By 1820, with four years left on that schedule, Dublin's trade interests petitioned Westminster for a delay and won one — 1 Geo. IV c. 45, tapering the protection toward 1825. Some of the city's own merchants had already made their peace with free trade as the coming order and wanted the delay dropped. The petition went forward regardless: a guild could lobby Parliament directly, plead its case in the language of commerce and precedent, while a combination of the men actually standing at the looms remained a crime.

4 Geo. IV c. 26 and 5 Geo. IV c. 22 cut the silk duties early. (What some later summaries call an 1806 announcement is a separate statute, Foster's Corn Intercourse Act — grain, on its own schedule, unconnected to the cloth timetable.) By 1824 the last protective duties came off, silk included, the same year the Combination ban lifted.

Article VII of the same Act had fixed Ireland's share of imperial expenditure at two-seventeenths, a ratio the merger of the two Treasuries in 1817 would soon test against real Irish tax revenue. The political economy taught in London at the time counted the folding of two economies into one free-trade zone as integration — the same reasoning that would have applied to any two markets merged into one, Dublin or otherwise.

By 1825 the hall on the Coombe was recorded as little used, its main room let to a Methodist congregation. George II stayed on the brick, the shuttle still carved into his hand, above a room no longer doing the work the statue commemorated. The guild, chartered in 1446, waited fifteen more years for a formal dissolution that had, by any working measure, already happened. Tenements stood where weavers' houses had stood a generation before.
