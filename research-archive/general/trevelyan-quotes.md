# Trevelyan: "Providence" / "judgement of God" quotations. Verification notes
Compiled 8 Oct 2026 (ET). Working files are in `/workspace/book/research/` (OCR text dumps, plus page scans in `scans/`).

Legend:
- **[SCAN-VERIFIED]**: I read the wording off a page image of the original printed source.
- **[SECONDARY]**: the wording rests on someone else's transcription or quotation. I have not seen the manuscript.
- **[NOT TREVELYAN]**: the words are not his.

---

## 0. Headline findings (read first)

1. **"The judgement of God sent the calamity to teach the Irish a lesson, that calamity must not be too much mitigated" is NOT Trevelyan's wording.** It is a paraphrase by the historian **Jenifer Hart**, "Sir Charles Trevelyan at the Treasury", *English Historical Review* 75, no. 294 (Jan. 1960), pp. 92–110, at **p. 99**. Her footnote 4 cites Trevelyan's letters of "6 and 9 Oct. 1846". Later writers lifted part of her sentence and printed it as a direct Trevelyan quote. Robin Haines, *Charles Trevelyan and the Great Irish Famine* (Dublin: Four Courts, 2004) traces the misquotation; secondary reports put the discussion at pp. 4–9, and say Hart told Haines in 1998 that she had misrepresented Trevelyan (p. 9). The author should **not** quote it as Trevelyan's.
2. **"...not the physical evil of the famine, but the moral evil of the selfish, perverse and turbulent character of the people" is NOT from the Monteagle letter.** Woodham-Smith, *The Great Hunger*, **p. 156**, gives it as a letter from Trevelyan **to Colonel (Harry D.) Jones, chairman of the Board of Works, 2 December 1846**. Her note (ch. 8, n. 28, p. 446) cites "Trevelyan to Jones, December 2, 1846, P.L.B. [Private Letter Books], Vol. X." The popular version starts "The *real* evil…". Woodham-Smith has "The *great* evil…".
3. The **genuine** 9 Oct 1846 Monteagle passage is the "direct stroke of an all-wise Providence" paragraph. Its manuscript (recipient's copy) is in the **National Library of Ireland, Monteagle Papers, MS 13,397** (see §1 for the /1 vs /11 discrepancy). It is not at the Bodleian or Newcastle, although a letter-book copy may be in Trevelyan's letter books (see §1).
4. The best-attested, fully verifiable "Providence" wording is in ***The Irish Crisis*** (Edinburgh Review, Jan. 1848, p. 320; Longman book edition 1848, p. 201). I checked it against page scans of both.
5. **No Trevelyan journal or diary exists for the famine period** (see §4).

## 1. Letter to Lord Monteagle (Thomas Spring Rice), Treasury, 9 October 1846

**Manuscript location:** National Library of Ireland, Monteagle Papers.
- The NLI's own finding aid (Collection List No. 122, https://www.nli.ie/sites/default/files/2022-12/122_monteaglepapers.pdf, pp. ~83–84) lists it under **MS 13,397/1**: "There is also a long letter (35 pp) from C. E. Trevelyan at the Treasury to Monteagle." It transcribes only the opening paragraphs.
- Peter Gray (via ricorso.net) and the sarkoups.free.fr transcription cite it as **MS 13,397/11** (also written "13,397.11"). **Discrepancy: confirm the folder number with NLI before citing.** The current finding aid says /1.
- A letter-book copy probably also exists in Trevelyan's semi-official letter books, which Hart used. History of those books: 38 vols, 1840–59, found at Wallington; temporarily deposited in the **Bodleian** c. 1958–67, which is where Hart and Woodham-Smith used them; deposited at **Newcastle University Special Collections in 1967**, Trevelyan (Charles Edward) Archive, series **CET/1** "Out letter books". The Newcastle catalogue (https://specialcollections.ncl.ac.uk/cet) notes "The letterbooks were previously held by the Bodleian Library, Oxford." I could not open the volume-level catalogue (blocked by a bot check), so the CET/1 volume number for Oct 1846 is unconfirmed. Woodham-Smith's "P.L.B. Vol. IX" covers Nov 1846, so Oct 1846 is probably Vol. IX.

**First publication:** Not Woodham-Smith. I searched the full OCR of her book and found no quotation of the 9 Oct letter. Hart (1960, p. 99) paraphrased it. The earliest verbatim printing I could identify is **Noel Kissane, *The Irish Famine: A Documentary History* (Dublin: National Library of Ireland, 1995), p. 51**, a documentary reader drawn from NLI manuscripts, so presumably transcribed from the manuscript. It is also quoted in **Peter Gray, "Ideology and the Famine", in Cathal Póirtéir (ed.), *The Great Irish Famine* (Cork: Mercier/RTÉ, 1995), pp. 86–103, at p. 93**, citing NLI MS 13,397/11; and in Haines 2004, p. 6. I could not open Kissane: the archive.org copy (irishfamine00noel) is lending-restricted.

**Key passage [SECONDARY]. Best reconstruction, with variants:**

> "I think I see a bright light shining in the distance through the dark cloud which at present hangs over Ireland. A remedy has already been applied to that portion of the maladies of Ireland which was traceable to political causes, and the morbid habits which still to a certain extent survive are gradually giving way to more healthy action. The deep and inveterate root of social evil remains, and I hope I am not guilty of irreverence in thinking that, this being altogether beyond the power of man, the cure has been applied by the direct stroke of an all-wise Providence in a manner as unexpected and unthought of as it is likely to be effectual. God grant that we may rightly perform our part and not turn into a curse what was intended for a blessing."

Wording variants between secondary transcriptions (none checked against the MS):

| Point | Kissane 1995 p.51 (as quoted by Gillissen 2014 n.10 and ricorso) | Gray 1995 p.93 (as quoted by ricorso) | sarkoups.free.fr full transcription |
|---|---|---|---|
| opening | (starts "I think I see…") | "I think I see… [...]" | "I must give expression to my feelings by saying that I think I see…" |
| "already been applied" | "has already been applied" | (elided) | "has been already applied" |
| "more healthy action" | "to more healthy action" | n/a | "to a more healthy action" |
| "Social evil remain[s]" | "social evil remains" | "Social evil remain[s]" (Gray's brackets) | "social evil remains" |
| Providence | "all-wise Providence" | "all wise Providence" | "all-wise providence" (lower case) |
| "unthought (of)" | "unthought as it is likely" (probably a dropped "of") | "unthought of as it is likely" | "unthought of as it is likely" |
| next sentence | (quote ends) | "God grant that we may rightly perform our part and not turn into a curse what was intended for a blessing." | same as Gray |

Recommendation: quote from Kissane p. 51 or Gray p. 93 in hand, or better, order a copy of the NLI MS. The safest wording is the column majority: "all-wise Providence … unthought of as it is likely to be effectual". Note that the sarkoups transcription has "I must give expression to my feelings by saying that…" before "I think I see". Unverified.

Fuller text [SECONDARY, sarkoups.free.fr/trevelyan1846.pdf, an anonymous web transcription with ellipses; the NLI finding aid confirms the opening paragraph nearly verbatim]: opens "My Dear Lord, I have had the pleasure of receiving your letter dated 1st instant…". The NLI finding aid reads "letters". It continues "The government establishments are strained to the utmost to alleviate this great calamity and avert this danger, as far as it is in the power of government to do so…", "It forms no part of the functions of government to provide supplies of food or to increase the productive powers of the land…", "…what I do not hesitate to call this defective part of the national character. The nobility and gentry have met in their respective baronies… calling upon the government to do everything, as if they have themselves no part to perform in this great crisis of the country." It ends: "The ministers of religion and especially the pastors of the Roman Catholic Church… have well performed their part; and although few indications appear… that the landed proprietors have even taken the first step of preparing for the conversion of the land now laid down to potatoes to grain cultivation, I do not despair of seeing this class in society still taking the lead which their position requires of them, and preventing the social revolution from being so extensive as it otherwise must become. Believe me, my dear lord, yours very sincerely, C.E. Trevelyan. Treasury, 9 October 1846."
- Context point, made by Haines and others: in this letter Trevelyan's scorn is aimed mainly at the **landed gentry**, not the starving poor.

**The phrases "judgement of God" and "too much mitigated" do NOT appear in any transcription of this letter.**

### Origin of the "judgement of God" sentence [SECONDARY: JSTOR scan text via sarkoups.free.fr/hart60.pdf]
Hart, EHR 75 (1960), p. 99 (her words, not Trevelyan's):
> "Trevelyan believed the Irish famine was the judgment of God on an indolent and unselfreliant people, and as God had sent the calamity to teach the Irish a lesson, that calamity must not be too much mitigated: the selfish and indolent must learn their lesson so that a new and improved state of affairs would arise.⁴"
> n. 4: "6 and 9 Oct. 1846."
(Hart spells it "judgment". She notes that the letter books were "now on revocable deposit" in the Bodleian.)

Trevelyan's 6 Oct 1846 letter, to Father Theobald Mathew according to Haines, reportedly says the opposite [SECONDARY, as quoted from Haines 2004 p. 5]:
> "Like you, I regard the prospect of Ireland with profound melancholy, but I fear less from the judgment of God than from the aggravation of man, owing to the ignorance, the selfish and evil passions of man."

How the conflated "quote" spread: Hart's paraphrase, plus Woodham-Smith p. 156 (Jones letter), got stitched together and attributed to the Monteagle letter. Examples: *The Independent*, "Historical Notes: God and England made the Irish famine"; Tim Pat Coogan, *The Famine Plot* (2012), pp. 63–4; numerous web pages. In the conflated version the "selfish, perverse and turbulent" sentence begins "The real evil…" and often capitalises "Famine".

## 2. *The Irish Crisis* (1848)

Editions checked:
- **Edinburgh Review**, vol. 87, no. 175 (Jan. 1848), Art. VI, "The Irish Crisis", pp. 229–320. Published anonymously. Scan: https://archive.org/details/sim_edinburgh-review-critical-journal_1848-01_87_175 (p. 320 = page/n319).
- **C. E. Trevelyan, *The Irish Crisis*** (London: Longman, Brown, Green & Longmans, 1848). Title page: "Reprinted from the 'Edinburgh Review', No. CLXXV., January, 1848". 201 pp. Printers: Harrison and Co. Scan: https://archive.org/details/irishcrisis00trevuoft (p. 201 = page/n206). Second copy: https://archive.org/details/irishcrisis00trev. Project Gutenberg #56727 transcribes the same 1848 Longman edition.
- (There is also a later reprint, London: Macmillan, 1880, with different pagination. I did not check it.)

### 2a. "direct stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence" + "sharp but effectual remedy" **[SCAN-VERIFIED]**
These come in one passage, the closing paragraph of the essay. Text below is from the **1848 Longman book, p. 201**. The paragraph starts on p. 199 with Spenser and the "appointed time of Ireland's regeneration"; the quoted part runs on from p. 200.

> "Now, thank God, we are in a different position; and although many waves of disturbance must pass over us before that troubled sea can entirely subside, and time must be allowed for morbid habits to give place to a more healthy action, England and Ireland are, with one great exception, subject to equal laws; and, so far as the maladies of Ireland are traceable to political causes, nearly every practicable remedy has been applied. The deep and inveterate root of social evil remained, and this has been laid bare by a direct stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence, as if this part of the case were beyond the unassisted power of man. Innumerable had been the specifics which the wit of man had devised; but even the idea of the sharp but effectual, remedy by which the cure is likely to be effected had never occurred to any one. God grant that the generation to which this great opportunity has been offered, may rightly perform its part, and that we may not relax our efforts until Ireland fully participates in the social health and physical prosperity of Great Britain, which will be the true consummation of their union."
> (Trevelyan, *The Irish Crisis*, London: Longman, 1848, p. 201)

**Edinburgh Review, Jan. 1848, p. 320** has the same passage with these punctuation differences (scan-verified):
- "subject to equal laws**:** and," (ER) vs "equal laws**;** and," (book)
- "the sharp but effectual remedy by which the cure is likely to be effected**,** had never occurred to any one." (ER: no comma after "effectual", comma after "effected") vs "the sharp but effectual**,** remedy by which the cure is likely to be effected had never occurred to any one." (book: stray comma after "effectual", none after "effected"; probably a compositor's slip, since both archive.org copies and Gutenberg show it)
- "this great opportunity has been offered may rightly perform" (ER, no comma) vs "has been offered**,** may rightly perform" (book)

The preceding sentences of the paragraph, book pp. 199–200 [OCR, cross-checked between two copies]: "…Our humble but sincere conviction is, that the appointed time of Ireland's regeneration is at last come. For several centuries we were in a state of open warfare with the native Irish… During all this time England reaped as she sowed: and as she kept the people in a chronic state of exasperation against herself, none of her 'good plots and wise counsels' for their benefit succeeded; for there was no want of good intention, and the fault was principally in the mistaken opinions of the age, which led to persecution in other countries besides Ireland."

Note how closely the published 1848 passage echoes the private 9 Oct 1846 Monteagle letter: "deep and inveterate root of social evil", "direct stroke of an all-wise Providence", "morbid habits… more healthy action", "God grant that we may rightly perform our part". This supports the authenticity of the Monteagle wording.

### 2b. Other providential lines in *The Irish Crisis* **[SCAN-VERIFIED in book; ER page from OCR]**
- Opening paragraph, book **p. 1** (ER pp. 229–30):
  > "Unless we are much deceived, posterity will trace up to that famine the commencement of a salutary revolution in the habits of a nation long singularly unfortunate, and will acknowledge that on this, as on many other occasions, Supreme Wisdom has educed permanent good out of transient evil."
- Book **p. 8** (ER p. 233):
  > "…but the only hope for those who lived upon potatoes was in some great intervention of Providence to bring back the potato to its original use and intention as an adjunct, and not as a principal article of national food; and by compelling the people of Ireland to recur to other more nutritious means of aliment, to restore the energy and the vast industrial capabilities of that country."
- ER p. ~258 (OCR only): the harvest of 1847, "by which the downward progress of that country has been mercifully stayed".

## 3. Other Trevelyan statements in this vein

### 3a. "selfish, perverse and turbulent": to Col. H. D. Jones, 2 Dec 1846 **[SCAN-VERIFIED as printed by Woodham-Smith; the MS is unseen]**
Woodham-Smith, *The Great Hunger: Ireland 1845–1849* (New York: Harper & Row, 1962), **p. 156** (verified on a scan of the Harper & Row 1962 US edition: https://archive.org/details/the-great-hunger-ireland-1845-1849-cecil-woodham-smith-cecil-w.-smith-cecil-blanche-fitz, p. 156 = page/n159). A secondary source also cites p. 156 for the Penguin reprint (1991/92). I did not check the London: Hamish Hamilton 1962 first edition.
> "These complications confirmed Trevelyan in his low opinion of the Irish. 'The great evil with which we have to contend,' he wrote to Colonel Jones, on December 2, is 'not the physical evil of the famine, but the moral evil of the selfish, perverse and turbulent character of the people.'²⁸"
- Note 28 (ch. 8, p. 446): "Trevelyan to Routh, December 8, 1846, B of W Corr. I, p. 326. / Trevelyan to Jones, December 2, 1846, P.L.B., Vol. X."
- "is" sits outside Woodham-Smith's quotation marks (it's her linking word). "famine" is lower case.
- Correct verbatim form for quotation: Trevelyan to Col. Harry D. Jones, 2 December 1846: "The great evil with which we have to contend [is] not the physical evil of the famine, but the moral evil of the selfish, perverse and turbulent character of the people." (Trevelyan Private Letter Books, vol. X; quoted in Woodham-Smith 1962, p. 156.) It has not been checked against the letter-book; Haines 2004 may print fuller context. Who the "people" are is debated; see the critiques citing Haines.

### 3b. "affliction of God's providence": early 1847 **[SCAN-VERIFIED as printed by Woodham-Smith]**
Woodham-Smith 1962, **p. 177**:
> "He felt he had done his best and could do no more. 'We deeply sympathise with you and other officers,' he wrote to the Relief Inspector, Colonel Douglas, on February 1, 1847, 'who daily have to witness scenes of heart-rending misery without being able to give effectual relief but, as justly observed by you, *we must do all we can and leave the rest to God*.' The thought that famine was the will of God was a consolation to him, and he hoped that the Catholic priests were making this clear. 'It is hard upon the poor people that they should be deprived of knowing that they are suffering from an affliction of God's providence,' he wrote.²⁴"
- Note 24 (ch. 9, p. 449): "Trevelyan to Douglas, February 1, 1847, P.L.B., Vol. XI. / Trevelyan to Hewetson, January 6, 1847, Ibid." So the "affliction of God's providence" line is most likely from **Trevelyan to Deputy Commissary-General Hewetson, 6 Jan 1847** (P.L.B. vol. XI), but the note does not say explicitly which letter it is.
- The italicised "we must do all we can and leave the rest to God" is Trevelyan quoting back Douglas's own words.

### 3c. Hart's other paraphrases (p. 99), not verbatim and not to be quoted as Trevelyan
"He regarded deaths by starvation as 'a discipline'…" (n. 5: 11 Feb. 1848). Only the two words "a discipline" are in quotation marks. "Similarly high prices were a check imposed by God and nature when there was a scarcity" (n. 6: Jan. 1847) is Hart's paraphrase.

### 3d. Routh / Charles Wood
I found no reliable verbatim Trevelyan letter to Sir Randolph Routh or Charles Wood framing the famine as Providence. Routh, not Trevelyan, wrote to Hewetson: "It is annoying that all these harbours are so insignificant. It shows Providence never intended Ireland to be a great nation." (Woodham-Smith 1962, p. 66, n. 26). **Do not attribute this to Trevelyan.** Woodham-Smith p. 155 quotes Trevelyan to Routh, 8 Dec 1846 ("We have reached an important crisis in our operations"; "We must at this stage throw all our strength into our inspecting machinery"), with nothing providential. The phrase often attributed to Trevelyan, the famine as "a mechanism for reducing surplus population", appears to be unsourced. I found no primary source for it.

## 4. "His journal"?
**No Trevelyan journal or diary for 1845–52 is known.** His surviving famine-era writings are: (a) the semi-official out-letter books (38 vols, 1840–59, now Newcastle University CET/1, formerly on deposit at the Bodleian); (b) official correspondence printed in Parliamentary Papers (Commissariat and Board of Works series, 1846–47) and Treasury records at TNA (T 64); (c) letters in recipients' papers (NLI Monteagle Papers; Hickleton/Wood Papers; Clarendon Papers); (d) *The Irish Crisis* (1848). None of the archive descriptions I saw lists a journal or diary.
Likely sources of the "journal" memory:
- *The Irish Crisis* first appeared in a **journal**, the *Edinburgh Review*, published anonymously in Jan. 1848.
- Woodham-Smith's acknowledgements mention "a manuscript **diary** written by the fifth Earl [of Courtown]", which has nothing to do with Trevelyan.
- Nassau Senior's *Journals, Conversations and Essays relating to Ireland* (1868).

## 5. Recommended wording for the book
- Quote the **published** words: Trevelyan, *The Irish Crisis* (London: Longman, Brown, Green & Longmans, 1848), p. 201; first published anonymously in *Edinburgh Review* 87 (Jan. 1848), 229–320, at 320: "The deep and inveterate root of social evil remained, and this has been laid bare by a direct stroke of an all-wise and all-merciful Providence, as if this part of the case were beyond the unassisted power of man." Fully verified.
- If using the private letter, cite NLI MS 13,397 (confirm /1 vs /11) via Kissane 1995, p. 51 or Gray 1995, p. 93, and check the printed page yourself.
- Do NOT quote "the judgement of God sent the calamity…" as Trevelyan's. If you mention it, attribute it to Hart (1960), p. 99, as her characterisation.
- If you quote "selfish, perverse and turbulent", attribute it to the letter to Col. Jones, 2 Dec 1846, via Woodham-Smith p. 156, with "The great evil…".
