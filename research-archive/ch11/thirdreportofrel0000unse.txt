REPORT. 


Relief Commission Office, Dublin, 
June 17, 1847. 
MAY IT PLEASE YOUR LORDSHIPS, 


WE have the honour to submit the third Monthly Report of our pro- 
ceedings under the Temporary Relief Act, 10 Vic., c. 7. 

Of the 2049 electoral divisions in Ireland, 1677 are now under the operation 
of the Act, and are distributing 1,923,361 rations per day, gratuitously, at an 
average cost of 24d. per ration; and 92,326 rations are sold. Of these 1677 
divisions under the Act, 1479 have received loans or grants, and 198 have not 
yet required advances. 

Of the remaining 372 electoral divisions not yet under the Act, we have 
reason to believe that 60 will not desire to come under the Act at all; and 
312 have not even yet sent in any returns or demands, although many of them 
are in much want. 

If the number of rations necessary for the returns still to be received shall 
bear the same proportion to the number of electoral divisions as those of which 
we already have cognisance, the entire number will be 2,388,475; and if the 
ordinary proportion for children at half rations be added, the number of 
persons to receive relief will be 2,729,684, of whom 2,622,684 will receive relief 
gratuitously. 

The time and opportunities have now been so ample for a suitable provision 
for the really destitute, since the Act was in force, that if the dreadful cases of 
actual starvation that have occurred in consequence of the calamitous failure of 
the potato crop are still to be found, the circumstance can only be occasioned 
by the neglect of local bodies, with whom rest the means of prevention. 

We believe, however, that these cases are now rare, and that an organisation 
is provided for arresting for the present so general a calamity with more com- 
plete effect than was probably ever before attempted. 

We will not deny that this result has not been attained without an enormous 
expenditure, many abuses, and the adoption of some inconvenient principles 
that it will be necessary hereafter to counteract. 

As the working of the measure becomes more and more developed, we are 
enabled to judge of the effect of the organization under which it is proceeding, 
and of its final results. 

Your Lordships are aware that the principle of the Act was to enable the 
different. electoral divisions to provide in each locality for the prevalent desti- 
tution by moderate exertions and a reasonable expenditure on their own part, 
aided by loans and grants from Government when required, and that the acts 
of these divisions were to be subject to the superintending control of a Finance 
Committee for each Union. 

The electoral divisions in Ireland vary in extent in area from 257 acres 
(Blackrock, in Rathdown Union) to 145,598 acres (Belmullet, in Ballina 
Union); in population, from 514 (Seacor, in Letterkenny Union) to 135,661 
(South City, Dublin); and have been valued at from 331/. 10s. (Mullaghderg, 
in Glenties Union) to 402,516/. 3s. 4d. (South City, Dublin). 

For the purpose of fixing upon an administration for relief in each division, 
the Lord Lieutenant, by his order of the 4th March, defined the classes of 
which it should be composed, consisting of some who had the greatest interest

in the welfare of the district, and of others who were likely to be most 
thoroughly acquainted with its wants. 

There are very few cases wherein, notwithstanding the difficulty existing in 
some localities of finding competent agents and machinery, the object of the 
Act might not have been attained with comparative facility, if the persons so 
designated had entered on the undertaking with earnestness and honesty of 
purpose. ‘ le 

We regret, however, to report that in very many districts such is not the 
case; and one result from the working of the measure appears to be manifest, 
viz., that for a general arrangement, a trustworthy local management, in most 
cases, cannot be ensured for smaller limits than those of a Union, although we 
are ready to acknowledge that in a great number of districts the complaint is 
not applicable. 

The Finance Committees, composed of from two to four gentlemen of each 
Union, have, with very rare exceptions, acted with zeal and intelligence, cor- 
recting in many instances errors of system introduced by the Relief Com- 
mittees; but they cannot always control details requiring very accurate know- 
ledge of the circumstances of persons and families. 

Where all classes of members of the Relief Committees have combined in 
good faith and spirit, the administration has been nearly perfect, affording 
effectual but reasonable relief to the really distressed, while maintaining a due 
check on the attempts at fraud and imposture that are naturally attendant on 
gratuitous distribution. 

From this degree of perfection, however, we notice the gradation to some 
counteracting spirit in the body ; a wrong feeling of one entire class ; a degree 
of apathy or neglect on the part of some, or the whole; a yielding to 
intimidation or seeking for popularity, leading to the abandonment of any 
attempt to check unreasonable demands on the supplies;: down to the last 
degree, namely, that of actual connivance at the grossest frauds, and misappli- 
cation of the means at their disposition, which last two are frequently effected 
by previously driving any well-disposed members from the Committee by 
violence or insults. 

In justification of ourselves, and as a proof of the difficulties we have to 
encounter in the endeayour to obtain a proper administration of the measure, 
we cannot refrain from laying before your Lordships instances of the kind of 
abuses practised. 

The first to be mentioned is the demand, in wholesale way, of rations for a 
greater number of destitute than there are individuals in the entire population 
of the district: this has occurred in several cases, and for thousands in excess. 

It is difficult to conceive the motive of such a reckless course. With some it 
appears to be an idea that, as an opening is afforded of drawing largely upon 
the public resources, it should be the endeavour of each locality to get as much 
as they can; while others, who are not blind to the future consequences of 
such proceedings upon property, would completely drain it for distribution 
among the poorer classes, under the delusive hope, it is presumed, that the 
latter would then be permanently raised to an easy and independent state of 
existence. 

In several instances the Government Inspecting Officer, on the occasional 
visits he is able to make to any one Committee (having perhaps the charge of 
sixteen or twenty), found no difficulty in striking off hundreds of names that 
ought not to have been placed on the lists, including sometimes those of 
servants, and men in the constant employ of persons of considerable station and 
property ; these latter are frequently themselves members of the Committees, 
and in some cases the very chairmen being magistrates, have santioned the 
issue of rations to tenants of their own of considerable holdings possessed of 
live stock, and who, it was found, had paid up their last half-year’s rent. 

Intimidation (a very common cause of abuses) has, in some places, been 
openly encouraged by members of Committees. It is a remarkable fact, how- 
ever, that wherever intimidation, or the violence of the populace, has been met 
with perseverance and energy by the Committees and the Inspecting Officers, 
it has in all instances been successfully resisted. 

When the efforts of the Inspecting Officers are unavailing in checking such 
abuses, the only remedy in our power is to reduce the amount of the advances

to what we see reason to believe, on the best information we can obtain, ought: 
af duly administered, to provide for the really destitute. 

Although we have no doubt that, even under such circumstances, our issue 
will be in excess, we shall expect that it will be in part misapplied, and an 
attempt made to charge us with a failure in supplying those who are really. 
distressed. 

In enlarging on these unpleasant facts we would beg that it may be under- 
stood that they are clearly exceptions, although sufficient in number to be 
deserving of notice, and to show in parts of the country a great want of the 
principles necessary for minute self-government. 

In other districts the measure is subject to the deceptions and petty frauds 
practised by many of the order immediately above the class of actually desti- 
tute, and which cannot entirely be checked by the best exertions of the best 
Committees. Thus even in the districts where the service is most correctly 
performed, the numbers on the lists will, no doubt, exceed those of the really 
destitute; and it will become very necessary, in any permanent arrangement, to 
provide for better security and tests than can be adopted under this Act. 

Of these we have little on which to place reliance, but the precaution of 
issuing cooked food, and we have every reason to believe this wherever it can 
be resorted to, to be a most useful and beneficial practice; nor have we heard 
any valid objection to it, since it has been understood to consist of a thick 
stirabout of meal, rice, &c., and not of thin soup. 

It has been reported to us, that the introduction of cooked food has been 
universally productive of the best effects on the health and appearance of the 
people. In several places, those who were at first clamorous against it, now 
request to receive no other; the ration, that as a pound of meal appeared so 
small, when mixed, well cooked, and swelled in its cold state to a mass of sub- 
stance weighing from three to five pounds, exhibits manifestly a very reason- 
able amount of subsistence, and is frequently acknowledged as such, even by 
those who have to depend upon it. 

In many respects, although subject to many of the evils and abuses to which 
we have before referred, we can report that the principal objects of the Act 
have been attained. 

The absolute starvation that was widely spread over the land has been gene- 
rally arrested, as has also the progress of the particular diseases engendered by 
the great destitution previously experienced. ; 

As an instance, we may give the Union of Skibbereen, with its dependant 
electoral division of Schull, the sufferings of which districts were so notorious. 

Although much wretchedness is still to be found in them, there is now a 
provision made for every part of the Union, the population is gradually amend- 
ing from their former emaciated state, and the people are beginning to turn 
their attention to future occupation and improvements, that may tend to their 
permanent employment and subsistence. 

In another Union, one of the very worst in Ireland, where five-sixths of the 
population require relief, there has been produced by a general distribution 
the same cessation of utter pervading misery, and in this latter case, we find 
that the cost of the present relief, extended as it is, will not much exceed two- 
thirds of that of the Relief Works, which left vast numbers of the most desti- 
tute totally unprovided, though assisted at the same time by large donations 
from the great charitable associations. 

Since our last Report, the temporary Fever Act, 10 Vict., cap. 22, has come 
into operation. 

We have reason to believe that the power given to Relief Committees to 
attend to the proper burial of those who die in a state of destitution, and to 
clear away nuisances and ventilate and cleanse cabins has been extensively 
acted upon, and, at a moderate expense, will tend to prevent the spread of the 
prevailing diseases. 

Two hundred and seven temporary hospitals have been directed by the 
Central Board of Health to be established, which are intended to serve so 
many of 481 contiguous electoral divisions as may require to use them. 

The measure has been too recently in operation for us to be able to report on 
its effects, except that in the North Dublin Union, by energetic proceedings and 
with the advantage of the resources of this city, some sheds were speedily

prepared, and patients in fever, for whom there was no previous provision 
were at once treated, and several discharged cured.

issued since the last Report.

) Relief Commission Office, Dublin Castle, 
Sir, 20th May, 1847. ; 


Tue Relief Commissioners desire to draw your attention to the following extracts from 
the Report of a zealous and intelligent Inspecting Officer. 

The Commissioners are, however, well aware that it has been quite out of the power of the 
ablest officers, in very many districts, to obtain the satisfactory results which have attended this 
gentleman’s efforts for carrying the Relief Act into operation. 

By order of the Relief Commissioners, 
W. Srantey, Secretary. 


“ T have now to state generally the course I have pursued, and the observations I have 
made as your Inspector. I found in some localities Committees under the old system, and in 
others little or no organization for purposes of relief. I called on all the resident gentry, 
clergy, and influential persons in the Union, and easily explained to them the superiority of 
the systematic and business-like arrangements now in force, over the ill-digested plans, varying 
with every locality, which they had previously adopted; so badly indeed had their business 
been conducted, that no Committee in this Union could have rendered an account of their 
expenditure in strict accordance with the rules under which they received donations in 
aid from Government. I need scarcely remark that the strict and periodical serutiny of the 
lists enjoined by your Regulations, exposed many. gross abuses which prevailed under the 
former system. 

“ The first consideration after assembling all the Committees according to the Lord Lieu- 
tenant’s Order of the 4th March was, how the funds required were to be raised. In some 
divisions there was a considerable balance in the hands of the former Committees, but others 
were not provided with any funds. In every case the Committees preferred voluntary subscrip- 
tions, in order to save the smaller and more distressed landholders from the pressure of a rate, 
and at the same time to avail themselves of the liberality of the Treasury in making free gifts 
in aid of private subscriptions, They have all either raised, or are actively raising contribu- 
tions sufficient, with donations-in aid from Government, to cover a period of three months. In 
the meantime the lists were prepared with great diligence in’ most cases, but with much differ- 
ence in expedition. , : ; . : 

“The Finance Committee have been very active in forwarding the relief measure, and 
have attended regularly, and, indeed; wherever called upon, and often at considerable personal 
inconvenience. minty. te ene 

*“ In many cases the rule of cooked food was at first dispensed with during the preparation 
of the kitchens. It is now, however, in almost universal operation, and highly approved 
of by many of those who were at first its most strenious opponents, and Iam happy to say 
that it has also found favour with with the recipients of charity. . The sale of rations at cost 
price, in aid of wages, has not as yet been thoroughly established, but it is gradually gaining 

round, 

ce However plausable may seem the objections which are constantly raised against these two 
rules, they ought to be strictly adhered to. I cannot see one reasonable or practical argu- 
ment against them; while, on the other hand, I know the numerous frauds, such as the sale of 
meal, or barter of it for tea, sugar, and tobacco, payment of debts, and even of rents with “it 
in lieu of money, and still grosser abuses, which | need not now particularise, not to mention 
injury to health, have been the common results of distributing raw food. In the other case, ° 
where the able-bodied in employment have received gratuitous supplies, or purchased at half 
price, a want of exertion in looking for good employment, and, in some cases, reduction of 
wages, or deduction of cottier rent from their earnings, have rendered the charity either injurious 
or unavailing. ; 

“ Throughout the entire Union emigration has taken place to an extent altogether unpre- 
cedented, and this, with fever and dysentery, has considerably reduced the population, which is 
still, however, far too numerous for the powers, of employment at present in existence, or for 
means of support extracted from the soil under its present management; and I should here 
observe, that neither the extent to which emigration is carried in this Union, nor the employ- 
ment afforded by drainage works under Mr. Labouchere’s Letter, reduced in any but a very 
trifling degree the amount of destitution which must be provided for, as it is the wealthier and 
more industrious class of small farmers who emigrate, and in the other case it is not those who 
are in absolute want who are employed. 

“ Notwithstanding the serious apprehensions which have been generally entertained con- 
cerning the tillage and cropping of the land, E am happy in being able to state that great 
progress has been already made. “Much land, indeed, which in other years would have been 
sown with oats, has not been so occupied this season, from inability on the part of the tenants 
to purchase large seeds, but the smaller seeds of green crops have been substituted, with, of 
course, more or less success, according to the condition of the land; some crop, however, will 
be found in all the land usually under tillage. A very small breadth of flax will be sown this 
year, principally owing to the general anxiety ta produce food of some sort or other in lieu 
of potatoes, the quantity. of which that has been planted has more than trebled the amount

anticipated, and, as far as we can judge of the earlier sorts, are at present in a healthy 
state, 

“ I have great reason to fear an unusual want of employment for the able-bodied, in districts 
purely agricultural, during the ensuing summer. ‘The farmers are executing all their work by 
the aid of their own families, or by mutual assistance rendered by-one to another. Their 
servant boys have been very generally discharged, and the servant girls have also been sent 
home to their parents; and I see no energy, except in very few instances, and to very small 
amounts, exhibited by proprietors in improvement, either by means of their own private money, 
or by availing themselves of the provisions of the Treasury Minute. In order to render the 
latter especially serviceable during the present year, I procured from the office of the Board of

Wherever in this Union employment has been freely given, the relief lists and estimates have 
been comparatively trifling. 

“ The Union workhouse has been closed for some time by order of the Poor Law Commis- 
sioners, a precaution imperatively required by the state of the poor within its walls; so much 
fever, and that of such a contagious character, was raging there that it might truly be called a 
pest-house, admission into which was dangerous in the extreme. 

“In conclusion, I beg to adduce a short calculation of the expense of the Temporary Relief 
Act in this Union, which I have always regarded as affording, in some degree, a trial test 
of the new permanent measure, and I think it will afford some proof that here, at least, the 
apprehensions so universally entertained of the ruinous expense of out-door relief have been 
unfounded, 

“ The expense of the Temporary Relief Act in this Union will be about 5000J. for three, 
monthe, and that at the very worst period when the workhouse is useless, and the relief list in 
consequence embrace all the destitution, This would not be five shillings in the pound per 
annum on the valuation of the Union; and if the annual expense of the workhouse, 4400/, per 
annum, be added, the entire cost would not amount to six shillings in the pound, even in this 
year of unprecedented distress.”

HOLDERS OF LAND. Cheaters, 
To the Inspectine Orricer of each Union. 


Relief Commission Office, Dublin Castle, 
Sir, 25th May, 1847. 
Tue Relief Commissioners receive many queries relative to the amount of holding of 
land that should disentitle a person from gratuitous relief, under the Act of 10 Vic., c. 7. 

The Commissioners do not consider that, under the circumstances of this country, the tenure 
of land should be held as any test for gratuitous relief, under a temporary Act in which there 
is no mention of so important a consideration, or that the relief which the Act directs to be 
provided should be made a means of summary ejectment, by which no advantage is gained to 
the Relief funds or measure. 

At the same time a holding of several acres affords reasonable cause for especial scrutiny 
and investigation, to ascertain whether the occupier is really in the destitute state which gives 
a claim to gratuitous relief. 

By Order of the Relief Commissioners, 
W. Srantey, Secretary.

. Crrcutar No. 45. 
APPLICANTS FoR RELIEF WHO Possess A Horse or Cow, &e. 
To.the Inspecting Orricer of each Union. 


Relief Commission Office, Dublin Castle, 
Sir, 25th May, 1847. 


-Tue. Relief Commissioners are frequently called upon to decide whether aman holding 
achorse, cow, or other property, for which he would obtain money, should get relief. 

Abstractedly speaking, a person who has any such property cannot be called destitute ; but 
if by retaining it there may be a prospect of his hereafter being enabled to provide for his own 
support, it would appear to be a reasonable and sound policy to afford him at once temporary 
relief, to avoid driving him into permanent destitution. 

A horse, unless.a valuable one, may be considered to be identified with the means of 
obtaining a livelihood, like the implements or machine of a mechanic, of which it would be very 
injudicious to deprive him. . 
By Order of the Relief Commissioners, 

W. Sran.ey, Secretary.

endeavour to point out the evils to which such practices must give rise. 

But in the wholesale fraud and plunder that has been reported from several Unions, (as in 
the above instances,) it is necessary to apply the only check in the power of the Commissioners, 
namely, withholding the funds, 

In all such cases where the Committees, by neglect, collusion, or intimidation, do not apply 
effective checks to the distribution, the Commissioners will seek for the best estimate they can 
of the gross number of the proportion of the population which may be considered to be really 
destitute, and will then make advances enly for the support of that number. 

Although the Commissioners are aware that much hardship may attend this system, as 
those who have occasioned its necessity, in many cases, will not shrink from the infamy of mis- 
applying the food, so as to leave many of the really destitute unprovided, it is the only resource 
left to the Commissioners ; and something must be attempted to check such thoroughly demo- 
ralizing influences, as would totally ruin the country, and eventually occasion unlimited misery 
and sutfering, 

With regard to the spirit of intimidation, the Commissioners believe that it is anything but a 
test of real want in the district ; and they’ consider the remark of one of their Inspectors to be

spirit and temper of the people being worse, is, in fact, an index of it,” ‘

To the Inspectine Orricer of each Union. 


Relief Commission Office, Dublin Castle, 
Sir, 15th June, 1847. 


Wuite the Relief Commissioners have to acknowledge the zealous and honourable 
services of a great number of the Relief Committees, anxiously endeavouring to afford reason- 
able and due relief, and at the same time to protect the rate-payers from overwhelming rates, 
and the country from the demoralising consequences of gross abuses,—it is a subject of great 
regret, that in some districts neglect, fear, or actual connivance on the part of the Committees, 
are leading to an opposite course of indiscriminate issues and reckless expenditure, 

The Inspecting Officers have, in many cases, by their own investigations and exertions, 
detected numerous frauds of this description, and have corrected them by causing even 
hundreds of names to be struck off the lists that never ought to have been introduced into them. 

These, however, are only partial remedies for evils that must be more extended. 

Therefore, when any Inspecting Officer feels a moral certainty that the issues are excessive, 
either in numbers or quantity, or are distributed without an efficient check to confine them 
to the really destitute, he will report the proportion which he may have reason to believe is 
clearly in excess, in order that the Commissioners may reduce the advances to the respective 
districts by that amount. : 

In cases where open force or violent threats are resorted to, so as to intimidate Committees 
from attending to the Commissioners’ regulations, it is uot an additional force, police or 
military, that should be applied for, to establish order ;—but the issues must be discontinued 
until the Inspecting Officer is assured that they will be received peaceably and gratefully; as 
it is unreasonable to require force to be employed for compelling people who are reputed to be 
famishing to receive wholesome food provided for their subsistence. 

By order of the Relief Commissioners, 
W. Srantey, Secretary.

In observing upon the duties of my department in the past month, I beg leave to report 
to the Commissioners that the several electoral divisions have been regularly supplied with 
funds (upon proper fortnightly estimates furnished) when it has been found that the directions 
of the Act have been fulfilled. 

By the annexed statement A, it will be seen that 1479 electoral divisions have been supplied 
with funds under the Act; the remaining 570 have either not entirely exhausted the funds they 
may have received from other sources, or are supporting their own poor without placing them- 
selves under the Act. Many of them, however, are conforming to the regulations, and trans- 
mitting the required returns, as shown by Statements B and C. 

The total number of rations being issued on the 5th instant, as shown by statement B, 
amounted to 2,388,475 daily, and as the half rations to children are included therein, it is 
equivalent to the support of 2,729,684 persons daily. 

The aggregate amount which has already been advanced under the sanction of the Treasury, 
whether by way of loan or grant, is 448,283/. 7s. 10d., as detailed against each Union in 
Statement D. 

As several Committees disregarded the instructions to them to render fortnightly accounts 
of the expenditure made by them under the Act, whereby the balance in hand remaining upon 
a former advance was kept from view, it was considered necessary to issue the circulars 12 and 
15 annexed, and in some few instances to return, under the direction of the Board, the estimates 
sent up, after such notice, without being accompanied by the previous fortnightly accounts. 

This is having its effect, as the accounts are now flowing into the office daily. 

At present, with my limited establishment, and the great increase of detail entailed upon 
my department by the Fever Act (10 Vic. c. 22), it is not possible for me to do more than 
examine the estimates as they are received in office; supply the divisions with money, record 
its issues, and collect the accounts of its expenditure. 

The examination of those accounts must, I fear, be deferred until the existing pressure for 
the supply of money is in some measure abated ; but the earliest attention possible shall be 
given to their investigation. 

In order to show the difficulty of obtaining accounts and vouchers from Relief Committees, 
I subjoin a few extracts (Paper G) from the reports of inspectors made to me, in reply to my 
constant references for their transmission, together with the continuation, “ Circulars 11 to L7, 
Finance,” referring to the details of my department.

36 APPENDIX TO THIRD REPORT OF 


(G.) — Exrracts from Inspecrors’ Reports to Accountant, showing the difficulty in 
obtaining Accounts from Retiree Commirrees; together with the further Finance Cir- 
CULARS issued, 

June 12, 1847. 


1. “ It has been found most difficult to get the clerks of Committees and treasurers to make 
up their accounts and vouchers in a perfect form. Ihave given instructions, and furnished 
specimens for entering the several items, but still the accounts, and vouchers continue to be 
sent in, with a few exceptions, unsatisfactorily.” 

2. “J have been endeavouring the last fortnight to get the accounts made up, and trust all 
will be clear, the cause being negligence and want of a knowledge of business on the part 
of both clerks and members of Committees.” 

3. “J have been doing all I can to urge the Committee to attend strictly to their instructions, 

particularly to the finance part, and either they do not read them, or from stupidity they do 
not understand them. I have never received one single paper, except from - — correct. 
You may imagine the sort of people some are, when I have been asked what a voucher 
was.” 
4. “Itis with the utmost difficulty I can get some of these Committees.to forward any 
returns; I have written half a dozen times for them to send in the fortnightly Returns with 
the receipts and vouchers; as yet, only one has been sent in correct. ‘They either forget 
one thing or the other, and appear to me to. know nothing, except how to destroy the forms.” 

5. “ Much procrastination is visible in forwarding the required returns by the clerks; the 
want of common attention and the stupidity is an obstruction; some ‘treasurers will not 
attend to the accounts, and I find much difficulty in getting any attention paid to the printed 
Circulars, which I believe are very little read. At the same time, I never cease to depict 
to the Committees the inevitable consequences.” 

6. “ The accounts I got, with one exception, were so imperfect, that I was obliged to return 
them: and until I have time to attend the arrangement of cash accounts with each Com- 
mittee, these fortnightly accounts cannot be procured in such a form as I could send you.” 

7. “I cannot transmit the enclosed Report, without remarking that I. have experienced 
great difficulty in making the clerks send in the Forms, marked F. Finance, properly filled 
up; and I have been engaged with the clerk of the Finance Committee during the most part 
of the past week in explaining the mode of filling up these papers, which I hope to have 
completed for all the electoral divisions in a few days.” 

8. « The cause of the delay in transmitting the accounts of the several electoral divisions, 
arises from the impossibility of getting them sent in, notwithstanding my utmost exertions, 
both personally and by writing.” 

“The clerks in rural districts ‘are for the most part totally inefficient, and the weekly 
stipend of 21s. is not sufficient to induce any person, accustomed to keeping accounts, to 
quit the towns and undertake such duties.” 

“ The Circulars 12 and 15, however (I am inclined to think), will effect much good, in a 
nearer approximation to correct accounts; but up to the the present, it has been the greatest 
difficulty I have had to encounter, and which I fear will still partially continue.” 

9. ‘I regret to confess that I can neither get the necessary documents sent forward with the 
punctuality nor accuracy that I would wish; it would be next to impossible to give you an 
idea of the trouble and difficulty that is experienced in getting clear and correct accounts 
from the Relief Committees, or indeed, to get them to render any accounts at all; I, 
however, hope they will improve.” 

10. « In transmitting the accompanying account, I beg to apologize for not having for- 
warded it before; but the fault does not rest with me, as I find it absolutely impossible to get 
correct or regular accounts from the several Relief Committees.” ida tac 

*“« The members of the Committees will not trouble themselves, and the clerks are generally 
very careless, and in many instances quite inefficient; so much so, that before I can procure 
anything approaching to correctness, I am usually obliged to return the accounts more than 
once.”’ 

11. “ Referring to Circulars No. 12, Finance, and Form 14, Finance, an Inspector reports 
that he has used every exertion to get the Accounts, Form F., Finance, properly arranged, for 
the purpose of transmission, but up to this time has not been able to obtain a perfect set for 
either fortnight ; he has been obliged to return the account furnished by Committees, they 
being in many cases deficient of vouchers; in others, réceipts for large amounts sent on 
unstamped paper; in fact, the great difficulty of obtaining correct accounts, as required by the 
Relief Commissioners, has occasioned the delay in forwarding them.”

Circutar No. 12.—Finance. 


Relief Commission Office, Castle, Dublin, 

May 25, 1847. 
As much inconvenience and delay arises from the manner in which the electoral 
division estimates, on Form D, are received at this oftice from the Finance Committees, the 
lief Commissioners desire me to call your attention thereto, and request your observance of 

e following rules, viz. :— 

1st.—All fortnightly electoral division estimates must be made up so as to end on the same 
day, according to the specimen sheet of Form A, viz.:—9th, 23rd May; 6th, 20th June ; 
4th, 18th July ; Ist, 15th, 29th August. 

2nd.—Separate and distinct estimates must be made out for each electoral division, 
although two or more divisions may form but one Relief District, and be controlled by one 
Relief Committee. 

3rd.—The whole of the electoral division estimates of the Union, arranged in alphabetical 
order, must be inserted in the abstract, Form E, when revised and approved of by the Finance 
Committee. If any division does not require funds, it is to be so stated against the name in 
such abstract, but the estimate is nevertheless to be sent up, marking upon it, in a legible 
manner, ‘* Sufficient Funds in hand,’”—so that the abstract may contain the estimates for all 
divisions for one and the same period. 

4th.—Each electoral division estimate is to be marked at the top on its face whether it is 
the Ist, 2nd, 3rd, fortnightly estimate, and so on, which has been sent to the Commissioners. 

5th.— Unless the accounts of cash received and paid for the preceding fortnight, showing 
the balance in hand on the Saturday, and abated on the face of the estimate, are regularly 
sent forward as required by Article 16, Part V., and Article 20, Part VII., money will not be 
forwarded as requested by the estimates. 

6th.—As some Committees appear to consider that an account will not be required to be 
rendered of the sums advanced by the Commissioners, as grants in aid of subscriptions or rates, 
I am directed to acquaint you that an account is to be kept and rendered of the expenditure 
of such sums, precisely as if they had been loans, One account only is required, which is to 
include both loans and grants. 

Lastly.—If the foregoing rules are strictly followed, no impediment will arise in the supply 
of money for the several electoral divisions, but if departed from, the Commissioners will 
withhold such supply, and the responsibility and consequences must rest with the Committees. 

By order of the Commissioners, 
R. M. Bromuey, Accountant.

Tue Relief Commissioners will send back every Relief Committee estimate that comes 
i into office without being accompanied by the Fortnightly Cash Account (Form F, Finance), 
and vouchers thereto ; as they are determined not to advance money without them. 
Every particular required in the estimate must be inserted in detail, otherwise it will delay 
the supply of money. 
«Circular No, 12—Finance,”’ must be strictly attended to. 


Further Explanation regarding Estimates and Accounts. 


It is very desirable for Parliamentary and other purposes, that the Relief lists, estimates, 
and accounts, should be made up to one and the same day. (See Circular No. 12.) 

It generally appears that a small balance remains in hand of a Relief Committee on the 
termination of a fortnight, which balance, together with credit, it is considered will carry a 
Committee through the greater part of the first week of the ensuing fortnight. 

The Finance Committee might examine the Relief lists and return them to the Relief Com- 
mittees: as well as revise and approve of the estimates and accounts, and send them to the 
Relief Commissioners by the middle of the week ; when, if found correct and complete, money 
would be sent down to meet the estimate before the expiration of the week—that is, the first 
week of the fortnight estimated for. 

This will be a fortnightly revision in the spirit and meaning of the Act. 

The issue of the rations, for the first two or three days of the fortnight, must be made upon 
the responsibility of the Relief Committee, but liable to revision on the return of the duplicate 
list from the Finance Committee. This will admit of the issue of food being continued 
without interruption. 

In making up the fortnightly Cash Account (Form F), it is only to include the actual 
receipts and payments within the fortnight, and the balance shown in the account must be the 
actual and bona fide balance in the hands of the Treasurer that can be produced, and which 
should be shown as abated from the amount of the estimate, “‘ Form D, Finance,” for the 
ensuing fortnight. If any bills remain unpaid at the end of the fortnight they are to be again 
estimated for, because the balance in hand is abated from the estimate. 

The Government Inspectors and Finance Committees are cautioned not to advance any 
money to an electoral division without taking a stamped receipt, signed by three membe: of 
the Relief Committee in the prescribed form,

Mucu inconvenience is experienced by the Commissioners not having before them 
information affording some definite anticipation of the probable expenditure for Relief. 

It is very essential that they should have an estimate for not less than three months for 
each electoral division ; and with it a statement of the per poundage proportion it bears to the 
Poor Law valuation, on which, if it should appear excessive, the Commissioners might decide 
what amount might be reasonably required from the district by a first rate, and what aid 
might then be recommended for a Government grant, 

This estimate should not be delayed, as it has been in many cases, for want of precise infor- 
mation regarding the number of men who may be expected to be employed by the Board of 
Works; but if that, or other information cannot be obtained, must be calculated approxi- 
matively, as well as may be, by the Finance Committee. 

Such Committees as have already sent in three months’ estimates, are requested to send in 
duplicates, marking upon them “ duplicates.” 

If there should be now reason to believe that the three months’ estimates already transmitted 
may prove insufficient, it will be a subject for a distinct supplementary estimate.
