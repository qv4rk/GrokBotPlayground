MAY IT PLEASE YOUR LORDSHIPS, 


WE beg leave to submit a Second Monthly Report of our proceedings 
under the Temporary Relief Act, 10 Vic., cap. 7. 

1248 electoral Divisions are under the direct operation of the Act, and many 
others have means remaining which enable them to hold back; many, how- 
ever still require an organized system of Relief, and we feel considerable dis- 
appointment that this progress should have been so slow, seeing no good reason 
why the measure might not, by this time, have been in full activity all over: 
the country. The means were to the best of our belief, within the reach of all 
the local bodies, through whose agency it was to be worked; and without 
whose assistance nothing could be done; but delays have arisen in many parts, 
from the great disinclination which seems to have been gradually arising 
against the commencement of an extensive system of out-door relief; and a 
vague impression of other more favourite systems of meeting the existing dif- 
ficulties. 

Some Commmittees have certainly worked with zeal and ability, and are 
proceeding to carry out the measure with charitable feelings, united to the 
firmness necessary to prevent its degenerating into abuse. 

Others are equally well disposed, but experience difficulties, which they 
cannot find means to overcome without inconvenient delay. 

Some are negligent from an aversion to this first essay of an out-dogr pro- 
vision, as above mentioned, or from a desire to force on a continuance of the 
system of Public Works as the general means of relief ; some from animosities 
and dissensions among themselves; and others from a want of willingness and 
energy to undertake the task. 

In addition to these causes there has: been, in many districts, an assumed 
excuse afforded for deferring the necessary proceedings under the Act, by the 
circumstance of old Committees, or parties having sufficient funds remaining in 
hand to provide for the most urgent wants of the moment. 

So long as hopes were entertained that, by the delay of the requisite pre- 
parations, the continuance of the expenditure on the Public Works could be 
enforced, on the plea that those works were to remain in activity till the 
other measure should be ready, a great negligence was observed in many 
districts; and it has been only by the determination shown in a considerable 
reduction of the works, that a certain degree of activity has at length been 
produced. 

This has been the only means in our power of stimulating the Committees 
to exertion, except by remonstrances through the medium of our Inspectors. 

Amidst the awful extent of distress that prevails generally over the country, 
and with extreme intensity in the west, and in parts of the interior, much 
alleviation has been afforded by the benevolent efforts of individuals, and of 
associations, and even by the active sympathy of foreign countries. 

The Public Works provided for a very large number of distressed persons, 
but as the extent of the calamity was gradually developed, together with the 
necessity for providing for the wants of millions of unfortunate beings, all 
these means have been found totally insufficient, and the Legislature has 
deemed it necessary to meet the emergency, by an arrangement as ; proposed by 
this Act, for the seeking out of every distressed individual. 

We make this statement to show the enormous arrangement that is 
dependent on this Commission, the great demands that arrangement must 
necessarily involve, and the real difficulties that must arise în its establishment, 
particularly when so much opposed to the desire and feelings of so many of

those on whose assistance we are dependent for carrying it out; and whoare čr 
only reluctantly led to lend that assistance, because no other means remain for 
saving numbers from absolute starvation. 

In the development of this great temporary relief operation, we are met by 
‘ many objections to our regulations, by assumed difficulties, and by remon- 
strances against the effects-of the Act itself; with the latter, of course, we 
have nothing to do, except to show that such effects, whether good or bad, are 
inherent in the measure, and not arising from our administration of it. — © 

The first and most reasonable complaint brought forward is, that it will 
have the result of feeding vast numbers of able-bodied men, who will be kept 
in idleness. 

We are hardly bound to explain why the insufficiency and enormous expense 
of a Public Works system have precluded the continued application of that 
remedy for this inconvenience; but we point out the anxiety of Government 
for the promotion of legitimate Public Works, and for the encouragement of 
private works, and we urge the importance of all proprietors, farmers, and of 
individuals in general, affording as much employment as may be in their 
power. 

It must be confessed that, notwithstanding every effort that can be made, 
there will still remain a vast multitude of able labourers to be fed without 
employment, and suggestions have been offered as to the propriety of esta- 
blishing some useful labour in return for the rations; but they all tend rather 
to the advantage of effecting that object than to the means by which it could 
be accomplished ; we concur in the principle, but consider that the result can 
be only very partially attained. This we have endeavoured to explain in our 
Circular of the 22nd of April. 

Demands continue to be strongly urged upon us to admit of the administration 
of relief in aid of wages, on the plea that a man cannot support a large family 
on his wages, although all above 15 years of age are allowed to be distinctly 
entered on the lists. i 

The principle we have laid down has been so completely understood and 
established in England, that it is only necessary to advert to it, in order to 
explain that, even under the present circumstances of Ireland, there is nothing 
peculiar to justify its relaxation; on the contrary, we consider that it is 
essential to avoid at this moment the introduction of a system which we are 
satisfied is in its nature pernicious, and calculated to add considerably to the 
future embarrassments of the country. 

Independent of the encouragement that would be given by this provision 
from a public fund to early marriages and their natural result, large families, 
without any prospective means for their support, it is not apparently perceived 
that the same argument would be equally available for the partial support of 
small families, or of the single labourer, and that wages would be no longer 
regulated by the ordinary competition of supply and demand, but would become 
absolutely at the caprice of the employer. 

If any deviation were made from this regulation at the present commence- 
ment of out-door relief, it would be found extremely difficult to rectify it here- 
after; and we should be accused, and with justice, of having been the means of 
inflicting a very serious permanent injury on the community. 

Dissatisfaction has been expressed from many parts at the inadequacy of the 
amount of ration that we have defined as the scale for distribution. Under the 
present dearth it would be impossible to afford relief to the degree that every 
charitable person would desire; it must be reduced to what is strictly neces- 
sary; and we have the authority of the Central Board of Health, to whom we 
have submitted our proposed scale, that it is reasonable, under the circumstances 
of the case. (Appendices A and B.) 

We find it difficult in many districts to obtain the establishment of soup 
kitchens, as the medium in part of relief. Dry meal is universally greatly pre- 
ferred, and, it is feared, chiefly on account of the opening it affords for abuses ; 
and many Committees, yielding to this popular feeling, or to avoid increased 
trouble, insist on its use. 

Other injurious deviations from our regulations are also frequently pressed 
upon us; and it may be deemed a matter of surprise that persons, who appear 
to have the greatest dread of the introduction of the measure of out-door relief, 
should, in many cases, advocate proceedings that would greatly add to its evils.

A very difficult question has been repeatedly submitted to us; viz., the extent 
of holding in land, or of property in stock, &c., that should disentitle a person 
to gratuitous relief. We consider that this can with reason only be dealt with 
according to each individual case. Abstractedly, it may be thought that the 
possession of any property should preclude a person from being considered 
destitute ; but, independently of the cruelty of this principle, it becomes a 
question of poliey, whether a man should be driven to permanent destitution, 
when, by some temporary relief afforded him, he might hereafter, by such 
reserved means, be enabled to gain an independent livelihood. 

The substitution under the Act, of relief by food for that by Public Works, 
as hitherto afforded, is extremely unpopular with all classes. There is a natural 
prejudice in favour of the useful effect that must, in a certain degree, be pro- 
duced by the latter, and for money payments; while the very great abuses to 
which the system gave rise are overlooked, as are also the demoralising effect 
of the semblance of labour for full amount of wages, and the impossibility by 
such means of meeting the extent of the emergency. 

The Public Works, as a measure of relief, are in course of rapid reduction, 
as the new measure is forced into operation; and we believe that the judicious 
arrangements of the Board of Works are effecting this object with firmness, 
combined with a due consideration for the circumstances of each locality, so as 
not to press on the distressed population more than is absolutely necessary to 
stimulate the Relief Committees to proceed with the means of distributing food 
under our Act. 

It is anxiously hoped that a large mass of the community, now absolutely 
requiring food, will gradually be absorbed by the prosecution of such works as 
can be recommenced on the legitimate basis of commensurate public utility, 
with a due amount of work for the expenditure, by undertakings by public 
Companies, Boards, and by the efforts of individual proprietors to establish as 
much labour as shall be in their power. 

The Legislature has been led by the circumstances of the times to deem it 
absolutely expedient to provide for the present distress by the searching measure 
of unproductive out-door relief. 

As Commissioners for carrying out this measure, our efforts are earnestly 
engaged in the endeavour to avoid the permanent evils to which it must be 
liable, but it is manifest that while we indicate the means by which, to the 
best of our judgment, this object is to be effected, the interests of the country 
are in the hands of local bodies and of local influences, in whom alone the 
power exists to bring it to the most favourable results of which it is sus- 
ceptible. 

While some are aware that such is the case, and are making laudable efforts 
to carry their districts through the existing calamity, as rapidly and with as 
little mischief as possible, we regret to say that there are others so short-sighted, 
so indolent, or so perverse, as to leave events to take their own course, and, conse- 
quently, to allow of mal-practices which they might prevent, or even to connive 
at them for the purpose of self-advantage. 

We accompany our Report by the Circular Orders we have issued since our 
last, and by a Summary of the Account branch. (Appendices C and D.)

Tue Central Board of Health having had under consideration your communications of 
the 2nd and 5th instant respectively, relative to the rations proposed by the Relief Commis- 
sioners to be allowed for adults, I am directed to state, for the information of the Relief Com- 
missioners, that the Board of Health are of opinion that the rations therein submitted to them

The Board of Health, however, suggest, that in all instances, where practicable, the food 
should be given out in a cooked form; as food, however good in itself, will, if not sufficiently 
cooked, not afford its full supply of nutriment, and it will, moreover, predispose to attacks of 
dysentery and diarrhoea, now so prevalent through the country. ; 

They likewise suggest that the food given out should be varied occasionally ; as, for instance, 
on one day that bread rations be given, and on another soup and bread, or meal and rice por- 
ridge, with bread, &c. They also recommend, as a general principle, that a considerable 
proportion of the nutriment should be administered in a solid or moderately consistent, rather 
than in a very fluid form; and they suggest to the Relief Commissioners the propriety of occa- 
sionally consulting the medical officers of dispensaries, as to the effects of particular articles of 
diet and the substitution of others, according as the preservation of health, or the appearance 
of any sickness may seem to demand such alterations.

Board of Health Office, Custom House, Dublin, 
SIR, llth May, 1847. 

I am directed by the Central Board of Health to acknowledge the receipt of your 
letter of Sth instant, relative to the scale of rations for adult destitute persons, and requesting 
the views of the Board as to the allowance proposed for children under nine years of age. 

The Board of Health are of opinion that the scale of rations proposed by the Relief Com- 
missioners, viz., half for persons under nine years of age, and full allowance for persons over 
that age is, under the circumstances, probably the best that could be devised; for although a 
more divided scale is useful and necessary in the dietary of large institutions, containing persons 
of different ages, the Board of Health fear that any attempt to carry out, in out-door relief, 
under 10th Victoria, chap. 7, a scale of rations more subdivided than that recommended by the 

Relief Commissioners would be attended with complication in the arrangements, and would be 
productive of unceasing disputes. 

Considering these circumstances, and the varying ages of children on the relief lists, the 
Board of Health concur with the Relief Commissioners in the scale proposed. 

The Board of Health now desire to draw the attention of the Relief Commissioners to the 
communication from the Board of Health, of 10th March, in which they recommended that in 
all instances where practicable the food should be given out in a cooked form. They are now 
from reports which have reached them, anxious to urge on the Commissioners the necessity of 
taking measures to have the recommendation acted on, without exception, in all instances. ‘The 
Board of Health feei it only necessary to observe, that they fear unless the principle be full 
carried into effect of giving out the food only in a cooked form, there will be always both an 
abuse and a serious evil of another kind to contend with. The abuse is the sale of raw meal 
for which purchasers are always to be found, to whom even the poor requiring the food oe 
themselves or family will dispose of it for money, tea, or tobacco; and the ST evil is the 
consumption of meal or rice, but more particularly Indian meal, in a raw* or badly cooked state,

The Board of Health wish to renew the recommendation in their previous communication, 
already referred to of 10th March, advising, as a general principle, that “a considerable 
portion of the nutriment should be administered in a solid, or moderately consistent, rather 
than in a very fluid form.” This principle is the more necessary to be attended to on the 
approach of warm weather, when soup, containing a large proportion of vegetable matter, is so 
liable to ferment and become sour. The Board of Health would, therefore, suggest, that instead 
of the soup ration, under section 26, consisting of a quart of soup and six ounces of bread, there 
should be hereafter substituted one pint of soup and sixteen ounces of bread, as the daily ration 
on soup days, or a ration of biscuits or stirabout, in cases where it may not be possible to 
supply bread or meat soup. 


As there are many different forms adopted for making soup, it may be useful to bear in“ 


mind, as a general rule, that a pint of meat soup should weigh nearly 14 |b. 

In serving out stirabout made of oatmeal, or of oatmeal and Indian corn, oatmeal and rice, 
&c., in order to prevent all disputes and frauds, to avoid the error of mistaking bulk for nutri- 
ment, and to insure that sufficient nutriment is given, the rations should be estimated not by 
the bulk when cooked, but by the actual weight of raw material; thus, as the allowance per 
day of meal for a ration is IIb., care should be taken that when cooked, each ration, whatever

ì Relief Commission Office, Dublin Castle, 
SIR, 22nd April, 1847. 


IT has been frequently represented to the Relief Commissioners, that it would be very 
desirable to obtain some useful labour from the destitute “ able-bodied ” persons who are to 
receive rations. 

The Commissioners acknowledge generally the justice of the principle, but feel that there 
are many difficulties in the way of carrying it out. 


1. The proportion of subsistence to be allowed is not such as to justify the demand 
of more than very moderate work, or of work in situations not very convenient to the 
persons receiving rations. This would prevent the principle from being generally 
applicable, and lead to a want of exertion on the part of the men that would be perhaps 
more demoralizing than relief without any work. 

2. The effect, for these reasons, would be quite inconsiderable in obtaining any 
practicable advantage to the district. 

3. No public department could undertake the arrangement for the work, which 
would consequently be left to the Committees themselves. 

4. No expense could be allowed to be incurred for tools, superintendence, &c., 
which would probably exceed any value to be obtained from the labour so employed, 
besides opening a door to abuses. 


The Commissioners at the same time conceive it possible, particularly in large towns, that 
aid might be given in labour towards many public objects, without incurring expense, or any of 
the difficulties stated: such labour the Commissioners would willingly encourage: they also 
think that all the mere labour attendant on the service of the Committees, with the distribution, 
and keeping order, should be performed in return for rations. 

By Order of the Relief Commissioners, 
W. STANLEY, Secretary.

IN reference to striking a rate for the purposes of the Temporary Relief Act (10th Vic- 
toria, cap. 7), the Poor Law Commissioners have to make the following observations: — 

1. Two concurrent rates would be as illegal now as if the Temporary Relief Act had not 
been passed: accordingly, in making a rate for the purposes of that Act, the arrear of the old 
rate should be carried forward, as is usual in making ordinary poor rates. 

2. It is understood to be the intention of the Relief Commissioners that no rate should be 
struck or resolved on for the purposes of the Temporary Relief Act which is not based on an 
estimate of expenditure for three months at least. Now it should be especially borne in mind 
that any such estimate should include the expenses of relief under the ordinary Irish Poor Laws, 
as otherwise the sum of money raised by the rate will be wholly inadequate to meet the liabilities 
of the Union. 

3. In resolving to make a rate, it is required by the Relief Commissioners that the proposed 
amount in the pound on the net annual value of each electoral division should be specially stated. 
Considerable delay may arise if this point is not attended to, inasmuch as a mere general 
resolution to make a rate without particularizing the precise poundage will be deemed insuffi- 
cient, and will be remitted to the Board of Guardians for revision. 

In conclusion, the Commissioners cannot too strongly impress upon Boards of Guardians the 
necessity of proceeding with the utmost energy in the collection of the rates. In addition to 
the expenses of maintaining the paupers in the workhouse, and of providing relief under the 
Temporary Relief Act, it is probable that the Guardians will soon have to incur other expenses 
for the relief of destitute persons not in the workhouse, and unless the collection of the rates is 
now pressed forward with vigour, the finances of many Unions will fall into a state of insolvency 
and confusion.

CircuLAR No, 35. 
To the INSPECTING OFFICER of each UNION. 


Relief Commission Office, Dublin Castle, 
SIR, llth May, 1847. 


Tue Relief Commissioners desire to circulate the explanations which they have given on 
inquiries addressed to them, in addition to those contained in Circular No. 22. 


RATIONS. 


1. If Committees find it convenient to issue rations of rice, or any other article of food not 
specified in the Commissioners’ regulations, and which shall be approved of by the Finance Com- 
mittee of the Union, they may allot the quantities in proportion to what they consider its nutri- 
tious qualities, so as to make up a ration equal to those defined in the Regulations, which have 
been laid down only as a general scale. 

2. As doubts have been expressed whether the rations defined in ‘the regulations, are, as by 
some considered, insufficient, or, as regarded by others, more than necessary, the Commis- 
sioners wish to have it known, that the Central Board of Health, which includes some of the 
highest medical authorities in Dublin, having been consulted on the scale of rations, and fully 
considered it, decided that under the circumstances of the country the allowances are reason- 
able. 

3. Various modes of reducing the allowances having been proposed by some Committees, 
through the adoption of scales of rations for families instead of allowances for the individual 
members, as fixed by the regulations, the Commissioners cannot sanction any of these scales 
for families. If the ration fixed by the regulations for each member of a family is only reason- 
able, the Commissioners cannot understand why the quantity for the whole family should be 
less than the total of the rations fixed for its several members. 

4. Any other than daily rations cannot be sanctioned, except as provided in the Instructions, 
part 4. The description of food which a sound policy requires to be adopted, precludes its dis- 
tribution in quantities exceeding daily wants. 

5. The daily attendance of able-bodied destitute persons, class 2, is indispensable, except in 
case of certified inability from sickness ; no other relaxation of the rule can be sanctioned. 

6. The holders of small quantities of land, who are destitute of means of subsistence, may, 
while actually cultivating their holdings, be supplied with rations through their wives or other 
members of their families, so that the cultivation of the land may not be interrupted by their 
having themselves to attend tor their rations. In these cases precaution must be used to prevent 
the rations being given in this way, when the cultivation of the land is not going on. 

7. On completing the tillage of their holdings, the occupiers, if still destitute, are to be re- 


| lieved as able-bodied destitute persons in class 2, if not employed as hired labourers, and the 


head of each family must then attend daily for the rations. 
8. If landholders be employed as hired labourers at wages insufficient to support their 
families, at high prices of food, they are to be included in class 4, and to be allowed to buy 
food from the Committee. 
9. Men engaged in preparing turf for themselves, (not for others at wages in any form,) 


- should be considered as in class 3, and with their families, if any, receive rations in the same 


manner, while so employed. 

10. The Commissioners receive frequent communications from Inspectors confirming their 
view of the great advantage of the distribution of cooked food instead of meal or rice in a dry 
state, as very far less liable to abuses and more nutritious when well made, (as it always ought 
to be.) than when prepared by any means of cooking which the destitute classes can possess. 
One intelligent Inspector writes : — 


“ In every electoral division in this Union, soup, with bread in addition, (the cost of one“ 


ration being just two pence,) has been unanimously decided on as the description of food to be 
given, or sold. 

<< At first meal was intended, and under the attractive influence of this description of relief, 
the lists were crowded with names that should not have appeared in them, but which their 
owners insisted on having placed there. 

“ Since the Union has resolved on soup distribution, many of those parties have indignantly 
withdrawn their names; the numbers on the Relief lists are reduced fully by three-fifths, and 
those alone are left who were contemplated by the Act, viz., the really indigent and destitute.” 


Price of Food to Class 4. 


11. Any food cooked in a boiler may be sold under first cost. 

12. The object of this rule is that labourers whose wages are insufficient for support of large 
families, with food at the trade prices, should be enabled to purchase from the Committees the 
same rations as are provided for gratuitous distribution ; but the price should not, as by some 
Committees proposed, be merely a nominal one. The intention is that the employed labourers 
described, should be enabled to buy cheap food; and this will be best carried out by providing 
them with cooked food, at a price equal to the cost of the rice, meal, &c., used, without the ex- 
penses of cooking or attendants. 

13. The price to be charged for rations is to be fixed by the Finance Committee; and stated 
at foot of the fortnightly list of sales to be made—Form B. 

14. In preparing the dazly rations the number of persons on all the lists, should necessarily 
be kept in view. 

RELIEF Lists. 

15. When cases of destitution shall arise after the passing of one set of gratuitous Relief 
lists, and before the approval of another by the Finance Committee, relief may be authorized 
by three of the Relief Committee, countersigned by the Inspector, and is to be allowed up to 
the next periodical revision, when it will be brought before the Finance Commiitee to sanction 
or refuse its continuance. 

16. In like manner, when persons on the gratuitous Relief Lists shall obtain employment, 
and the issues of gratuitous rations to them must cease, they may be allowed to buy rations with 
the sanction of three members of the Committee. 

17. For all intermediate changes three members of the Relief Committee may sanction the 
adoption of the form of relief applicable to the cases, which at the end of the fortnightly period 
will appear on the lists and estimates for the Finance Committee. 

18. In placing persons on the lists who have sons or daughters above the age of fifteen years, 
the two latter when unemployed must apply for relief separately, and, if admitted to receive it, 
be registered separately; so that when employed they shall not get rations gratuitously through 
the parents who may be uneniployed. Ali destitute persons over fifteen years of age must 
attend daily for the rations, except in case of certified sickness. 

19. When unemployed parents apply for relief, and reside with sons or daughters who are 
earning the means of subsistence, the existence of a necessity for relief in such cases should be 
closely investigated. The fact of a son’s being in employment should not, however, of itself be 
considered as excluding the parents, if unemployed, from gratuitous relief. 

20. There is no rule laid down in the Temporary Relief Act or the Commissioners’ Instruc- 
tions in reference to the residence of persons who may apply for relief. The Act is intended 
to provide for the relief of the same persons as the Relief Works’ Act was designed to provide, 
either directly or indirectly ; and as the relief is to be as general as the existence of a necessity 
for it, there does not appear to be any reason for assuming that many cases will occur, except 
in sea-ports, where relief will be required by others than persons usually resident. Where 
such cases do exist, there is no remedy applicable to them which Committees can be authorized 
to adopt. Actual destitution should in every case be relieved; and when the number of 
strangers requiring relief in any town shall be considerable, it will be for the inhabitants to 
devise some mode of inducing such persons to return to their own localities, and provide them 
with means for the purpose. Such arrangements, however, cannot be mixed up with the relief 
operations under the Act. 

21. It is open to Committees to refuse relief to persons in the Ist and 2nd classes, whenever 
there may be room for them, and they can be received in the Union workhouse.

Committees, in respect to the clause of the Relief Act which prohibits their being concerned 
in contracts. Any member who has a doubt as to his liability under the Act should take legal 
advice. 

28. When magistrates or principal officiating clergymen who are members of Relief Com- 
mittees, desire to nominate substitutes, according to the Lord Lieutenant's General Order of 
the 4th of March, a written statement of such nomination should be sent to the Inspecting 
Officer of the Union, giving the surname and christian name of the substitute, with the name of 
the electoral division for which nominated ; and nominations by clergymen of the Established 
or Roman Catholic Church, should also give the name of the parish in respect to which the 
appointment is to be made. A magistrate of any part of Ireland, who has property in an elec- 
toral division of another county, may nominate a person to act in his place. 

29. The person nominating (except in the case of clergymen) cannot act with his substitute 
for the same electoral division; but the principal clergyman of two or more parishes, or of dis- 
tricts hitherto formed in a town, any part of which may be in the same electoral division, can 
himself act for one, and nominate an assistant in respect of each other parish or town district. 
The parishes referred to in the Lord Lieutenant’s General Order of the 4th March, are those 
laid down in the Ordnance Survey. 

30. The Presbyterian clergymen who are members of Committees, are the seniors by ordina- 
tion respectively officiating in each division. The district for which a Presbyterian clergyman 

' may officiate is not referred to in the Lord Lieutenant’s Order, but only the electoral division ; 
and when a question may arise as to the district, it is to be determined by the locality of the 
church in which the senior clergyman officiates , the division in which that church is being the 
one which such clergyman is to represent in the Relief Committee. 

31. It appears to have been intended by the Act of Parliament that the Finance Committees 
should be a controlling power over the Relief Committees; but the Commissioners consider 
that the object of the Act is to prevent great abuses, not to interfere too much with the discre- 
tion of Relief Committees, particularly when they are composed efficiently, and where there is 
reason to believe that they are proceeding with good intentions. "The Commissioners have 
themselves acted on this principle, even yielding at times to opinions contrary to their own, 
when not involving any matter of much importance, rather than check the energies of a zealous 
body. In cases where differences arise, (which hitherto have been very rare,) the Inspecting 
Officer should endeavour to reconcile them.

Tuer Relief Commissioners having received representations of the insufficiency of the 
rations which the Relief Committees are required to distribute, more especially the allowance 
for children under nine years of age, they have again consulted the Commissioners of the 
Central Board of Health, under whose approval the scale of rations was adopted; and a further 
expression of their opinion that it is sufficient, under the circumstances of the country, is con- 
veyed in the letter, a copy of which is annexed to this Circular. 

It will be observed that the Commissioners of the Board of Health consider the issue of the

rations in a cooked form, indispensable not merely to check abuse, but to prevent injury to 
health consequent on the use of raw or insufficiently cooked food. 

The cost of cooking and the difficulty of distribution are commonly urged in justification of 
the practice of issuing the food in a raw state; yet if the number of persons desirous to obtain 
food in that shape and ready to solicit it, as an object of general advantage or profit, without 
being in a condition to require relief, shall be contrasted with the number in actual want, the 
use of cooked food will be found to be much the cheapest form of relief; but it is not merely 
with a view to saving that this course is enjoined, although economy is a most important object 
in all public affairs, but more particularly in the present calamitous condition of this country ; a 
paramount consideration, however, is the necessity of preserving the health of the population, 
and checking the progress of the diseases now prevalent, by providing the destitute classes with 
food in a wholesome state. For this object the Relief Commissioners and the country at 
large must necessarily look to the Relief Committees, and it is to be hoped that mere local 
difficulties will not be regarded as insurmountable, when it is of so much consequence that they 
should be overcome.

Board of Health Office, Custom House, Dublin, 
SIR, llth May, 1847. 


I am directed by the Central Board of Health to acknowledge the receipt of your letter 
of the 5th instant, relative to the scale of rations for adult destitute persons, and requesting the 
views of the Board as to the allowance proposed for children under nine years of age. 

The Board of Health/are of opinion that the scale of rations proposed by the Relief Com- 
missioners, viz., Half for persons under nine years of age, and full allowance for persons over 
that age, is, under the circumstances, probably the best that could be devised ; for although a 
more divided scale is useful and necessary in the dietary of large institutions, containing persons 
of different ages, the Board of Health fear that any attempt to carry out, in out-door relief 
under 10 Vic. c. 7, a scale of rations more subdivided than that recommended by the Relief 
Commissioners, would be attended with complication in the arrangements, and would be pro- 
ductive of unceasing disputes. 

Considering these circumstances, and the varying ages of children on the Relief lists, the 
Board of Health concur with the Relief Commissioners in the scale proposed. 

The Board of Health now desire to draw the attention of the Relief Commissioners to the 
communication from the Board of Health of the 10th March, in which they recommend that 
in all instances, where practicable, the food should be given in a cooked form. They are now, 
from reports which have reached them, anxious to urge on the Commissioners the necessity of 
taking measures to have the recommendation acted on, without exception, in all instances. ‘The 
Board of Health feel it only necessary to observe that they fear, unless the principle be fully 
carried into effect, of giving out the food only in a cooked form, there will be always both an 
abuse and a serious evil of another kind to contend with. The abuse is the sale of raw meal, 
for which purchasers are always to be found, to whom even the poor requiring the food for 
themselves or family will dispose of it for money, tea, or tobacco; and the serious evil is the 
consumption of meal or rice, but more particularly Indian meal, in a raw* or badly-cooked 
state, which will predispose to and aggravate dysentery and diarrhoea, already so prevalent 
through the country. | 

The Board of Health wish to renew the recommendation in their previous communication 
already referred to, of 10th March, advising as a general principle that “a considerable portion 
of the nutriment should be administered in a solid or moderately consistent, rather than in a 
very fluid form," this principle is the more necessary to be attended to on the approach of 
warm weather, when soup containing a large proportion of vegetable matter is so liable to 
ferment and become sour. The Board of Health would therefore suggest that instead of the 
soup ration under section 26, consisting of a quart of soup and six ounces of bread, there should 
be hereafter substituted, one pint of soup and sixteen ounces of bread, as the daily ration on soup 
days ; or a ration of biscuit or stirabout in cases where it may not be possible to supply bread 
or meat soup. 

As there are many different forms adopted for making soup, it may be useful to bear in 
mind, as a general rule, that a pint of meat soup should weigh nearly l4lb. 

In serving out stirabout made of oatmeal, or of oatmeal and Indian corn, oatmeal and rice, 
Ae., in order to prevent all disputes and frauds, to avoid the error of mistaking bulk for nutri- 
ment; and to insure that sufficient nutriment is given, the rations should be estimated not by 
the bulk when cooked, but by the actual weight of raw material ; thus, as the allowance per 
day of meal for a ration is llb., care should be taken that when cooked, each ration, whatever be 
its bulk, should contain the equivalent of llb. of raw meal.

10th May, 1847. 


I wave the honour to lay before the Commissioners the following observations upon and 
summary of the duties performed in my department during the past month. 

At the commencement of the month of April the estimates from the Electoral Division Com- 
mittees were received in office but slowly. They were generally made out upon a correct prin- 
ciple, and reported by the inspecting officers to have been framed upon the revised lists of 
destitute persons. 

Towards the end of the month the estimates were received in rapid succession; but notwith- 
standing their increased numbers and the precaution necessary to be observed in admitting 
estimates involving so large an expenditure, the whole of them were, with scarcely an exception, 
laid before the Commission within three days of their receipt in office,—the far greater majority 
of them on the following day. 

Generally the same day on which the estimates have been approved of, His Excellency the 
Lord Lieutenant has been moved to issue his warrants to the Board of Guardians for paying 
over the amounts of the same to the treasurer of the Finance Committee of the Union. 

In order further to expedite the supply of money to the Committees, the inspecting officers 
have been required, on transmitting the estimates, to report the state of the funds, if any, in the 
hands of the Boards of Guardians of the Unions ; which report having been considered, and no 
funds appearing on hand, the Lords of the Treasury have been moved to sanction advances by 
way of loan on credit of rates about to be struck, without waiting for the Boards of Guardians 
answering the warrants. 

By this means the Commissioners, in most instances, have been prepared to remit money to 
the several electoral divisions, immediately upon the receipt of the certificate from the inspector 
that the Board of Guardians had resolved to strike a rate in accordance with the 16th clause of 
the Act. 

In some few instances partial delay has arisen from the want of such certificate; but I have 
not failed in such cases to remit money on the day of its receipt, and I am happy to say that 
the financial arrangements throughout the country work with so much ease that [ have been 
enabled to forward money to the several Committees daily as the sums were approved of by the 
Treasury, although atter banking hours, and the business of commercial matters had closed. 

The following will show the progress made financially in carrying the Act into effect :- — 

Ist. The number of Electoral Division Committees, whose estimates have been approved of 
in the month, amount to 1248. 

2nd. Of which His Excellency has been moved to issue warrants for 1117. 

3rd. The authority of the Lords of the Treasury has been asked for advances to be made as 
loans on the credit of rates to be levied to the amount of 711,4597. for 971. 

4th. Under whose sanction 797 have been placed in funds by the Commission in 110 Unions 
out of 130. ; 

The Treasury’s sanction has not yet been received to the last list of electoral divisions sub- 
mitted, which will account for the difference in the number placed in funds as compared with 
those submitted for approval. I beg to subjoin a statement, showing in what manner the Com- 
mittees propose to raise the funds necessary to meet their estimates ; also a statement of the 
balances in the hands of Committees and the number of persons now receiving relief either gra- 
tuitously or by sale, and the amounts advanced to the respective Unions to the present time.
