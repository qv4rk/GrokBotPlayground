their superintendence during the preceding month of February, the serious atten- 
tion which the great importance of the subject demands. 
The number of persons employed upon the Relief Works at the close of each 
week, during this month, are stated to have been as follows:

- It is manifest from the general tenor of this Report, and more particularly from 
the following passage, that all effectual control over the increase in the number of 
persons employed, and over the manner in which the work is executed by them, 
has, for the present, been lost. 

‘ We feel that as long as the number of the destitute continue to increase as 
“ they have done, at the rate of about 20,000 persons per week, and as long as 
* every person sent to the work must be employed, and, no matter how idle, 
“cannot be dismissed, except on account of insubordination or outrage, the 
“ overseers, the greater number of whom have been necessarily taken from the 
“ surrounding country, are unable, perhaps sometimes unwilling, to enforce regu- 
larity or system in works executed by a mass of unskilful, and frequently weak 
* and even dying creatures.” 

It has already appeared from a variety of evidence which has been laid before 
this Board, that the continuance of the Relief Works materially interferes with 
the prosecution of the ordinary agriculture of the country, and this conclusion is 
strongly confirmed by the following, among other passages of the Report. 

« Every exertion must be made to encourage private employment, but the 
“ people evince no readiness, at present, to avail themselves of it, nor will it be

“ wholly practicable at once to convince them that the works which remain in 
“* progress are no longer to be considered merely as Relief Works.” 

“« Employment from the farmers is, at present, offered in vain, because the men 
“ employed on the road-works, on acceunt of their numbers, can rarely be suffi- 
“ ciently attended to, and are consequently suffered to idle ; as well as because where 
“* task-work has been introduced merely as a means of affording higher wages, in the 
“ first instance, the ordinary prices of manual work have been gradually increased 
“ to enable the increasing numbers of unhappy, ill-fed, or unwilling labourers, still 
“ to earn from ls. to 1s. 6d. per day.” 

The state of things above described has already engaged the serious considera- 
tion of this Board; and being strongly impressed with the necessity of imposing 
an effectual check upon it, their Lordships caused a communication to be 
made to the Board of Works on the 16th ultimo, on the subject of the discon- 
tinuance of the Relief Works, and, on the 2nd instant, the attention of the Com- 
missioners of the same Board was strongly called to the financial consequences of 
permitting the expenditure for the Relief Works to continue at its present rate 
after the expenditure for the new Relief Act shall have commenced. 

Sir George Grey has likewise addressed a letter to the Lord-Lieutenant, repre- 
senting to his Excellency the urgent importance of proper measures being taken 
on these important subjects. 

The matter has now become of so grave and critical a nature, that if the number 
of persons employed on the Relief Works is not reduced in time to remove all 
obstacles to the usual amount of labour being employed in preparing the land for 
the next harvest, evils must ensue which, while they would be painfully felt 
throughout the whole kingdom, would in Ireland produce calamities greater even 
than those which have been hitherto experienced. 

It appears to my Lords from the Reports and other documents which have been 
laid before them, that all the instructions which have been from time to time 
issued, either to reduce the number of persons upon the works, or not to 
employ persons rated at £6 and upwards, and every other regulation of similar 
import, have been found utterly ineflicacious to check the inordinate increase of 
persons upon the Relief Works, and that a large proportion of the Relief Committees 
have reem mended for employment upon those works, in considerable numbers, 
persons having no claim whatever to relief, and have latterly abandoned all attempt 
to investigate the claims of the applicants. 

My Lords are, therefore, convinced that the only effectual mode of checking the 
expenditure, and inducing the Committees to perform their duty in revising the 
lists, is by positive instructions, issued on the direct responsibility of Her Majesty’s 
Government, to limit the number of persons employed, leaving it to the local 
Committees, with the assistance of the Inspecting Officers of the Board of Works, 
to carry those instructions into effect, according to the circumstances of each 
district. 

With this view their Lordships direct that notice be immediately given, that from 
Saturday, the 20th March, the number of persons employed on the Relief Works 
will be reduced by not less than 20 per cent., and that the remainder will be further 
diminished by suecessive reductions, in the proportions, and at the times to be 
hereafter fixed by this Board, until the new system of Relief, under the 10 Vice. 
cap. 7, shall have been brought into full operation. 

The necessary steps for carrying this arrangement into effect, will be taken by 
the Board of Works in the following manner :—Persons holding ten acres of land 
and upwards, are to be discharged from the 20th of March, even if they should

Their Lordships have watched with the deepest interest the exertions which the

made which will be necessary to overcome the difficulties attending the present 
critical period of the Relief operations.

REPORT. 


Relief Commassion Office, Dublin ; 
April 10, 1847. 
MAY IT PLEASE YOUR LORDSHIPS, 


As Commissioners for Relief under the Act of 10 Vict. cap. 7, we sub- 
mitted a preliminary Report of our proceedings on the 27th February, 1847. 
and in compliance with your Lordships’ instructions, we now transmit our first 
monthly Report. 

Our earliest attention was turned to the formal nomination of the superin- 
tending Committees over Unions, called the Finance Committees, each to be 
composed of from two to four members, selected from among resident gen- 
tlemen, having an interest in the district, and capable of undertaking the 
charge. 

It was from this body, (of which the Government Inspector, is an ex-officio 
member) that we hoped for zealous exertions, in speedily assembling and subse- 
quently directing the Relief Committees for electoral divisions. 

In the selection of the individuals to be recommended to the Lord Lieutenant 
for Finance Committees, we could not apply a principle of representation for 
different parts of the Union, because the number of eligible persons, being so 
small, it was necessaty to look as much as possible, to the convenience of resi- 
dence, that would facilitate regular attendance. Nor could we always appor- 
tion the nomination between creeds and parties. 

We consider, however, and have found, as far as we can at present judge, that 
these distinctions have become far less essential than was formerly the case, 
and that there is a spirit of social feeling generally prevalent now amongst 
the people of this country, that (laying aside exclusive motives,) leads them 
anxiously to co-operate with each other, for mutual support and assistance, as 
members of one commanity; and independent of any partial considerations. 

In calling into operation the direct working Committees for Relief, by 
electoral divisions, (of which there are 2049 in Ireland, and they would all, 
with a very few exceptions, come under the operation of the Act,) the 
individual nomination necessary for Finance Committees, appeared hopeless ; 
the Lord Lieutenant therefore, on the 4th of March, issued a regulation (of 
which we transmit a copy) constituting the Electoral Committees by classes, 
adapted we believe, as far as could be, to a due representation of all, and to the 
constitution of a body that ought to be calculated to work out the essential 
duties with which the Relief Committees are charged. 

It was to be expected that this classification might have the effect of intro- 
ducing some individuals who would be, from character or qualifications, 
objectionable, or incompetent; and: that it would cause the omission of others, 
whose exertions would be most valuable; still it was considered, that the best 
basis would thus be established for a general organisation ; and that improve- 
ments could be subsequently introduced, under the general powers vested in 
the Lord Lieutenant. 

Acting under this view, we have been so desirous of giving this system a fair 
trial, that we have almost universally declined recommending a compliance 
with the very general demand that has been made for the addition of members, 
until there should be proof of its necessity, in order to avoid the great increase 
of members on the Committees, which would have been the effect of our 
acceding to many of the propositions submitted to us. 

We do not however deny that in many cases additions will be necessary 
for the better working of these Committees; but they will now be proposed 
more deliberately, to meet the exigency of each case; and not in the sweeping 
manner in which they have been hitherto offered to us.

8 FIRST REPORT OF THE RELIEF COMMISSIONERS. 


On the 11th and 16th of March, orders were gazetted, directing that Relief 
Committees should be constituted, according to the general order of the 4th of 
March, in each electoral division of ninety-six Unions, since which time the 
other Unions have been successively organized, and at present there are J*inance 
and Relief Committees in one hundred and twenty-scven Unions, leaving only 
three into which the Act has not been introduced, namely: Antrim, Belfast, 
and Newtownards. 

Early in the month, we issued instructions to the Government Inspectors, 
and for the guidance of the Finance and Relief Committees, accompanied by 
forms of accounts, and other information, that we trusted would be found 
sufficiently explicit. 

The object at which we aimed, in these Instructions, was to lay down such 
general principles as could be made applicable to all, affording every aid by 
forms and details, that would facilitate the operations; at the same time 
leaving to the energies and discretion of Committees, all that could be done to 
most advantage by local arrangements, or that would be subject to the many 
varieties that local circumstances would require. 

Of the principles laid down by our Instructions, two, to which we attach 
much importance, have called forth several remonstrances. 

Ist. The prohibition of administering relief under the Act in aid of wages. 

- 2nd. The restriction to the sale of food, under prime cost, with the exception 
of soup. 

Many plausible arguments are submitted to us for a relaxation of these 
rules, but, while we admit that they may be attended by occasional inconve- 
niences, we are persuaded that these evils almost universally arise from the 
attempt to maintain and perpetuate unsound principles, and not from our regu- 
lations, which would tend to place the social system of labour on a wholesome 
basis. 

It might be productive of many advantages if work useful to the community 
could be arranged by the Committees, under sanction*of the Government 
Inspector, for the employment of the able-bodied poor, who must be fed; but it 
would be attended with much difficulty, and we would not recommend an 
attempt to put such a proceeding under the charge and management of any 
public department. 

The correctness of the principle of restricting the sale of food to a minimum 
of prime cost, including expenses, has been sufficiently proved, and it has also 
been laid down in your Lordships’ Instructions. 

The various accounts that must necessarily be entered into for an expendi- 
ture so large, scattered, and detailed as is required by the generally distressed 
state of the country, under the provisions of the Act, together with the vast 
number of forms and documents that it became necessary to arrange for these 
accounts, and for regulating the distribution of relief, engaged our early con- 
sideration. These were speedily arranged under the superintendence and 
direction of our accountant, Mr. Bromley, and they have been distributed 
generally as fast as they could be prepared in print and binding, particularizing 
for first issue those Committees that were reported to be ready for them. 

The distribution is now nearly completed, and your Lordships may conceive 
the mass of labour, in many branches, on this head, when informed, that these 
documents comprised upwards of 10,000 books, 80,000 sheets, 3,000,000 card 
tickets, and that their gross weight was not less than 14 tons. 

Mr. Bromley, on 22nd March, made, for the information of your Lordships, a 
full Report of the proposed system for the entire of the accounts of the Com- 
mission, which has since received your Lordships’ sanction. 

The organization being thus completed so far as concerned the necessary steps 
from this office, the further working out of the measures rested with the Com- 
mittees and Government Inspectors. We could do no more than stimulate their 
exertions, which we have done by repeated circulars, and by clearing up the 
misapprehensions and anticipated difficulties submitted to us, which were 
numerous in the aggregate, owing to the number of bodies with which we have 
to deal. 

The principal subject of reference has been the desire to add, by specific 
nomination and recommendation, to the lists of Relief Committees. 

This desire has been so general, that, if at once admitted, the proposed 
systematic classification laid-down in the Lord Lieutenant’s regulation of the

4th March, would have been nullified; and we should have been engaged in 
a labyrinth of individual applications, recommendations, and _reproaches. 
We have, therefore, required that an attempt should be made to constitute 
working bodies under the arrangement of 4th March; and that, on the actual 
proof of their insufficiency, the absolutely necessary number of additions 
should be proposed, under the recommendation, in each case of the Finance 
Committees. 

A very considerable degree of impatience has been expressed in many parts of 
the country, that the new measure of relief should be brought into immediate 
operation, in order to make that general provision for the destitute population, 
which could be only partially effected under the Public Works’ system; and 
this impatience has led to pressing applications being made to us for the advance © 
of sums of money, in anticipation of the completion of forms that would be 
proceeded with in the mean time. 

It was out of our power to comply with such demands; but we have been 
willing to forward the proceedings for any part of an Union, the necessary forms 
for which could be perfected without waiting for the entire. 

Other attempts have been also made to obtain advances on the production of 
some very imperfect and summary forms and estimates; but we feel bound to 
require that the documents should be completed in such a manner as to afford 
a full justification of the expeuse to be incurred, in spite of the accusation 
brought against us, that we are withholding food from the starving population, 
an accusation which, if it can be made with justice in any quarter, must be 
applied to those who leave unfulfilled the conditions required of them. 

We find almost universally that the members nominated to Finance Com- 
mittees express great zeal and willingness to undertake the arduous duties 
confided to them. 

It is reported by our Inspectors, that the same feelings actuate the Relief 
Committees in many cases, while in some others we are “informed that much 
delay and suffering is likely to be occasioned by such a degree of apathy and 
unwillingness to attend to the business, as can only eventually be remedied by 
the addition of new members, or perhaps, in extreme cases, by the establish- 
ment of a new committee altogether, to perform the duties, that those already 
constituted have thus neglected. 

However zealously the several Committees and our officers may have acted, 
it is manifest that the accumulating extent of the calamity, and the precautions 
necessary to ensure regularity, and some check upon so enormous an expendi- 
ture, requires more time before relief by food can actually be administered, 
than is consistent with the natural impatience created by the emergency. 

The making out of the lists requires much labour and arrangements for its 
subdivision, and we have received up to this time but a limited account of 
demands for advances, and those generally for such small sums for the com- 
mencement of operations, as could be justified by documents rapidly got up for 
portions of the districts, of which we have transmitted to your Lordship the 
details. 

This inconvenience will, or ought to be, alone experienced on the first pro- 
ceedings, for those, if correctly carried out, will subsequently require only occa- 
sional partial alterations; and there will thenceforward be a power of fore- 
seeing every requisite for future advances. 

Another cause of local delays has arisen from circumstances affecting some 
of our inspectors. 

Major Parker and Captain Drury caught the fever at Skibbereen and 
‘Kinsale, while in the zealous discharge of a most arduous duty, to which they 
fell victims. 

The gentleman sent to replace Major Parker resigned his situation, after 
being there 24 hours; and finding that in that unhappy § district the business was 
in consequence so much retar ded, and that severe disease was rapidly in- 
creasing among a throughly dispirited population, we have sent two inspectors 
to that ‘union, one of them a staff surgeon; considering that although his busi- 
ness would not be professional, medical knowledge and experience would, in 
the Government Agent, be incidentally of great service. 

Two more of our Inspectors have also been incapacitated at different stations 
by attacks of fever, of whom one required to be relieved permanently, and one

temporarily. We have also thought it advisable to send an army surgeon to 
Kinsale. 

One Inspector has resigned from dissatisfaction at his situation. 

Considering the large number of persons still employed on the Public Works, 
notwithstanding the recent reductions, and the amount of funds, and of food, 
that has recently been distributed by charitable associations and individuals, 
and by donations from Government, we regret the vast extent of the existing 
calamity, indicated by the still pressing demands from all quarters for the 
speedy operation of the Act; and we cannot but fear, that in spite of every 
effort to prevent abuses, and to stimulate the country to self exertion, the weight 
upon this measure of relief will be found exceedingly heavy. 

One favourable feature has recently appeared amidst the gloom of this dis- 
tressing scene, in the amount of imports of food, and the consequent reduction 
of prices. This comes most opportunely for the working of this Act, as it will 
enable the Committees to lay in, with greater facility, stocks of provisions, at a 
much lower rate than could have been anticipated. 

Your Lordships are aware that the relief that we are now administering is 
not only of a temporary character, but necessarily of a nature contrary to all 
sound principles of policy. 

It is not for us to propose how it is to give place to more permanent 
measures, or better principles; but we shall endeayour to avoid as much as 
possible any proceedings calculated to continue arrangements in the social 
system that might ultimately lead to lasting evils, considering it more desirable 
to incur partial present inconvenience (under the penalty of the reproaches it 
will occasion) than to be the means of introducitfg measures productive of 
eventual injury to the community.

15. The Finance Committees are requested particularly to direct their attention to principles 
of action on the part of the community, on which depend a great deal of the amount of present 
suffering ; and will, it is hoped, use their utmost influence to cause them to take a right 
direction for the future welfare of the country. 


Firstly. Any impression which may have arisen, in consequence of the new Bill for the 
Temporary Relief of the Destitute Persons in [reland, that individuals and associations, 
especially those of the localities, may discontinue their voluntary efforts, should be strenu- 
ously counteracted. If such a principle were to be extensively acted upon, the relief 
would no doubt eventually fail. Neither would it be consistent with the feelings of 
humanity to leave a population, in the lowest state of want, to the limitted pittauce that 
could alone be doled out under a provision by law; a course that would tend to raise up 
feelings of despair and alienation, if not animosity, as the natural consequence in the minds 
of the suffering poor at finding themselves thus abandoned. 


Secondly. A feeling may arise among the labouring classes, that it is now less necessary 
for them to rely on their own energies, as they may in future expect to obtain support 
from the public. Independent of the degradation that every individual must incur who, 
by reason of his own neglect or idleness, requires subsistence from the public, he should be 
made aware how miserable and scanty that subsistence must be, if it can be continued at 
all, and how unmanly it is to abandon his independence, and all hopes of bettering the 
condition of his family. 


Thirdly. There may be an attempt, on the part of farmers and occupiers of more land 
than they can cultivate by their own labour, to make out the amount of wages which 
ought to be paid to their hired labourers by the gratuitous distribution. This has been 
adverted to in the instructions to the Relief Committees, and however plausible may 
appear to be the pleas in favour of it, should never be admitted, even under the present 
circumstances. It is pregnant with danger, and has already been the cause of very great 
evils in England. If such a system were allowed, it would become general; and the 
injustice of thus defraying, generally by the public, part of the cost of the produce of a 
business, in contemplation of profits, would tend to far greater injury than would arise 
from even partial non-employment, in the few cases where the entire family could not earn 
the day’s wages of one efficient labourer. 


Fourthly. The despondency at the loss of the potato, it is understood, has, in some 
instances, led to a reckless spirit of neglect of the cultivation of many of the small holdings, 
partly arising, perhaps, from a want of knowing in what manner to procure a crop that 
shall be a substitute. This is a matter of considerable difficulty, and is only to be dealt 
with in detail, according to the especial circumstances that in each district lead to this 
neglect. _It is an object of vital importance to prevent, as much as possible, any available 
land remaining unproductive; and even should there appear to be difficulties respecting 
the provision of seed, it is incumbent on the Committees, as well as on all persons of 
property and intelligence in the country, to exert every influence in their power to provide 
a remedy for the evil, by investigations and endeavours to remove whatever may be its 
cause. 


6. The system of public relief works, so injurious and demoralizing, when carried to the 
extent that, owing to peculiar circumstances, they have now reached, must be discontinued 
forthwith, and labour must be reinstated in the wholesome condition of being applied solely to 
objects that shall be indisputably worthy of it, and of being only paid for in proportion to the 
full value of work done. Under those conditions, it will be most desirable to encourage it in 
the greatest degree, both by public and private works, arranging for reducing the operations on 
the former during the periods when labour is wanted for tillage. 

7. It is, however, from private employment in the cultivation and improvement of land that 
the greatest benefit is to be derived; and the members of the Finance Committee are earnestly. 
requested to promote this desirable object by every legitimate means—by their gwn example— 
by their personal influence—and by endeavouring to convince employers of labour that it is for 
the common interest of all to prevent the peasantry from acquiring a habit of dependence on 
gratuitous relief. 

8. ‘The Finance Committees will fix days for periodical meetings for despatch of business, 
which should not be less frequent than once a week. 

9. The Poor Law establishment will be directed to render them every accommodation and 
assistance in its power. Any other establishment expenses that may be necessary for carrying 
on the duties will be paid out of the Relief Fund, and must be on the most moderate scale, and 
submitted previously for the sanction of the Commissioners. 

10. The Commissioners will receive with respect any suggestions for carying out the relief 
measures which the Finance Committees may be inclined to submit to them through their 
inspecting officers, and will actively co-operate in all that shall be in their power, and that may 
appear to them to be really beneficial.

On the Manner of Keeping the Accounts. 


11. The duplicate relief list must be transmitted by the Relief Committee to the Finance 
Committee every fortnight, with the several alterations and additions that they may have con- 
sidered it necessary to make. The Finance Committee must verify and correct this list when 
considered necessary, approve of it by the signature of the Chairman after the last name on the 
list, and return it to the Relief Committee. 

12. The relief list for sale of rations to poor families whose earnings are insufficient to pur- 
chase enough food, on account of the high prices, must be prepared in duplicate by the Relief 
Committee, and sent every fortnight to the Finance Committee for approval, both as to the 
number of rations, and the rate at which each ration is to be sold during the ensuing 
fortnight. ’ 

13. The account book of rations sold, and the amount received for the same, which is to be 
kept by the clerk to the Relief Committee, must be inspected, from time to time, by the Finance 
Committee, or their clerk, to ascertain that the number of rations issued to any individual, and 
the rate charged for rations, corresponds with the relief list approved of for the time by the 
Finance Committee; also, that the daily receipts are correctly charged in the fortnightly 
accounts of the Relief Committee. 

14. An estimate of the amount required by each Relief Committee must be forwarded to 
the Finance Committee acting for the Union, every fortnight, showing the value of the 
provisions (required to provide the necessary number of rations) authorized to be issued by 
the relief list, which must be ascertained by adding the totals of the several pages together ; 
with the cost of the establishment, and every other local expense whatever; showing also the 
manner it is proposed the funds should be provided. The Finance Committee must revise 
these estimates and calculations, and decide on the amount to be provided. 

15. The clerk of the Finance Committee is to collect the several estimates when so revised, 
and form them into one general estimate, for transmission, accompanied by the Relief Committee’s 
estimates, to the Relief Commissioners, stating the time when the amount will be required ; which 
general estimate must be signed by two members of the Finanee Committee, one being the 
Government Inspector. 

16. The Finance Committee must require from the Relief Committee a separate account 
every fortnight, for each Electoral Division within its province, to accompany the estimate 
before stated, showing the receipts and payments within the fortnight, and the balance remain- 
ing in hand (if large, the issues from the , Finance Committee must be regulated accordingly), 
which aceount must be supported by proper receipts and documents, bearing due authority for 
the sums paid. The clerk to the Finance Committee must examine very closely the several 
items of this account, and point out to his Committee any unusual or unauthorized expendi- 
‘ture, in order that explanation may be called for from the Relief Committee. ‘The account and 
vouchers must be retained by the Finance Committee until required by the Relief Com- 
missioners. 

17. The treasurer of the Union will be the treasurer for the Finance Committee, but must 
keep the two accounts separate and distinct; to whom will be remitted, from time to time, 
such sum or sums,of money as the Relief Commissioners may think fit, founded upon the 
foregoing estimates, whether as loans on the credit of rates, or as grants in aid of rates, or 
contributions for the purposes of the Act. Such officer will, therefore, be the person to be 
named in the warrant of the Lord Lieutenant, referred to in the 8th Clause of the Act. 

18. The treasurer may only make payments out of such money, under the signatures of the 
Chairman of the Finance Committee, for the time being, and of the Government Inspector, 
jointly—retaining the direction as his authority for the issue, and taking the receipt of the 
party to whom he pays the amount, according to the same regulations as required under the 
Poor Law Aet, as a voucher to obtain credit from the Relief Commissioners. 

19. The treasurer of the Union, acting as treasurer for the Finance Committee, will be 
required to transmit monthly to the Relief Commissioners, in Dublin, an account, within three 
days after the expiration of each calendar month, showing his receipts and payments on 
account of the Committee, and the balance remaining in his hands, which account must be 
accompanied by complete vouchers in support of his receipts and payments; a duplicate thereof 
must be sent to the Finance Committee, which is to be signed by the chairman, and also for- 
warded to the Commissioners. The clerk of the Finance Committee must keep an account- 
book of the receipts and payments, as a check upon the treasurer, with which he is to com- 
pare the duplicate account. 

20: When the Guardians shall not have money in hand, sufficient to meet the amount men- 
tioned in the warrant of the Lord Lieutenant, referred to in Part IV., but shall have resolved 
on making a rate for the purposes of the Act, the Government Inspector of the Uuion shall 
certify to the Relief Commissioners, that a resolution has been passed, and that, to the best of 
his belief, the Guardians are proceeding with all possible despatch to make and levy such rate; 
when the Commissioners will, if they shall think fit, make such loans on the credit of the rates 
so about to be made, as shall be authorised by the Lords of the Treasury. 

21. In transmitting to the Relief Commissioners the respective estimates and accounts, as 
well as observations arising out of them, it is requested that the word “ Finance” be inserted 
at the left-hand corner of the address on the letter.

Reeutations for Retrer Commitrees under the Act 10 Vic. cap. 7. 


1. The Legislature has placed the responsibility of providing means for the relief of destitute 
persons under the existing calamity on Committees to be. constituted for electoral divisions, 
subject to, the regulating control of a Finance Committee for each Union. : 

2. The funds to be applied to the service will consist of local or other subscriptions, with 
donations from Government, and, where these may be insufficient, of any monies in the hands 
of the Poor Law Guardians that can be so appropriated ; also of rates to be laid on the elec- 
toral divisions where they may be needed; in aid of which rates, according to the” exigency 
of the case further donations will be contributed by Government. 

3. The funds derived from subscriptions and donations that remain in the hands of the 
present Relief Committees will generally be available for the Committees under this Act; but 
should there be any difficulty owing to the different arrangement of districts, the Commis- 
sioners recommend that they should be allocated to the new districts in proportions, under the 
arbitration of those Finance Committees acting for the greater part of the old district. 

4. Relief is to be given exclusively by the distribution of food; gratuitously to those who 
are absolutely destitute, and by sale at reasonable prices to those who are employed or have 
means of purchasing. 

5. A Government Inspector will be appointed to each Union; he will be an ex-officio mem- 
ber of every Committee constituted under the Act within the Union; he will aid the Com- 
mittees, especially those of Finance, with his advice and exertions, to cause the provisions of the 
Act to be carried out in the most effective manner, and will be the medium of communication 
between those Committees and the Commissioners.. 

6. The Electoral Division Committees, which will be the active relieving bodies, will each, in 
the first instance, elect a treasurer from their own body, and appoint a clerk, who will give his full 
time to make up lists of the poor, to keep accounts, and generally for office or other work, at such 
moderate salary as the Committee may suggest, and as shall be sanctioned by the Commissioners. 

7. Every other assistance that will be absolutely required must be provided from time to 
time as the necessity for it shall arise, subject in every case of what may be deemed standing 
establishments, to the express sanction of the Finance Committee. 

8. The first duty of the Electoral Division Committees will be to draw up lists of the persons 
requiring relief, in the forms furnished to each Committee, in as perfect and complete a manner 
as possible, and with a strict and uncompromising regard to truth. ” 

9. ‘The persons requiring assistance may be classed under the four following heads, each of 
which will have to be dealt with on distinct principles :— 

Ist. Destitute, helpless, or impotent persons. 

2nd. Destitute able-bodied persons, not holding land. 

3rd. -Destitute able-bodied persons holders of small portions of land. 

4th. ae ee employed at wages insufficient for their support at very high prices 
of food. 

10. To the three first named classes it is necessary to extend gratuitous relief. 

11. With regard to the fourth class, that of employed labourers, assistance can only be 
afforded by selling food of a cheap description, for it must be a fixed rule to refuse to them 
and their families any gratuitous supplies. If this were once to be deviated from, and wages 
were to be made up from any general funds, it would be the foundation for an evil of the greatest 
magnitude, Gratuitous relief being procured and distributed with the great economy demanded 
at the present crisis, the wages that a man ought to be paid for a reasonable day’s work will 
place him and his family in a better condition than those who are dependent on gratuitous relief ; 
and as it may be expected that all the next agricultural produce will fetch high remunerating 
prices, it would be particularly unreasonable to provide in any way from a public fund towards 
the obtaining of that produce. No business ought to be carried on that cannot support itself. 

12. The Commissioners consider this rule, of not allowmg gratuitous relief to be in aid of 
wages, so essential, that they must require the most watchful and strict revision of the lists, for the 
purpose of at once striking off the names of all persons who shall be employed for hire either 
on public or private works, or who shall refuse employment at a reasonable rate of wages, 

13. The second class should be considered as temporary, being subjected to a careful 
revision; and the fact of non-employment may be ascertained by requiring the daily attendance 
of the able-bodied member or members of the family at the food depot. 

14. The net annual value of the holdings of occupiers of land, according to the Poor Law 
valuation, might form one criterion of the claim to relief for individuals of the third class, and 
be regulated by the Finance Committees according to the condition of the several districts. 
Should any individual exceptions be recommended by the Electoral Division Committee, it 
should be on strong grounds that must be fully explained. 

15. To entitle any holder of land to gratuitous relief it should be absolutely required of him 
to proceed diligently with the cultivation of his holding, or of its preparation for cropping ; 
and a fixed time should be given to him for the purpose, as a check against his leaving it to 
be imperfectly tilled by the females of his family, or by occasional work himself, while he may 
be in some other employment for wages. When he has executed that duty he must be con- 
sidered as falling into the second or fourth class. 

16. These lists are to be made out in duplicate, and one must be forwarded to the Finance Com- 
mittee, assembled at the chief town of the Union, accompanied by an estimate of the amount that 
will probably be required to provide for the extent of relief in the ensuing fortnight, including 


the cost of the means of preparing and distributing food, also of establishment, and every other

local expense whatever, showing also the manner in which they propose that the funds should be 
provided. The fortnightly revision:of these lists and estimates must in like manner be submitted 
to the Finance Committee. The Relief Committees will also offer suggestions as to the mode 
in which they consider the supplies can be best obtained and distributed. 

17. The Finance Committee will take every means in their power to verify and correct these 
lists, and when approved return them to the Relief Committees, on which will depend the pos- 
sibility of providing in any degree for the existing deficiency of food. Any fraud attempted in 
them must be considered as a crime of the deepest character, tending to increase the effects of 
the present state of destitution. 

18, The rated value of the holdings of small farmers and occupiers will be examined by the 
clerks of the Unions, and certified by them upon the lists. 

19. The Finance Committee will then revise all the estimates and calculations, and decide 
on the amount to be provided for a given period, which should not be less than three months 
if a rate is to be struck. 

20. On the signing of the amended list and.estimate, the Inspector of the Union is to report 
to the Commissioners the amount, and when funds are required from the Board of Guardians 
of the Union the Lord Lieutenant will be moved to cause a warrant to be issued for the 
amount, or such proportion as his Excellency may approve, requiring the Board of Guardians 
to pay the sum mentioned within a stated time, to the person who shall in the warrant be 
named to receive it. If the Guardians shall not have money in hand sufficient for the purpose, 
they are to levy the sum required as part of the poor’s rate. (See Part V.) 

21, The Finance Committee will, at a very early period, take into deliberate consideration 
the manner in which food of the kinds best suited to the purpose and most easily obtained can 
be provided, either by adopting the recommendations of the Relief Committees, or what they 
mav consider more eligible. 

22. It is probable that the more the provision can be sought for in mass, and for large quantities 
under one arrangement, the cheaper will be the supply, and the less liable to occasional defi- 
ciencies. When the Relief Committees are to provide the materials for the food, they will by the 
Finance Committee with the money; aud when the latter shall order and collect the materials 
for the food, it will be distributed to each Relief Committee under the arrangement which the 
superior body shall direct. In either case the subsequent distribution will be arranged by the 
Relief Committee according to the lists, and to the regulated quantity for each individual. 

23. It is impossible to prescribe minutely what may be the arrangements for providing, 
preparing, and distributing food, best suited for each locality ; but the following are some of 
the principles which will apply te all. 

' 24, One of the first and most important matters to be attended to, by whatever Committee 
as to provide the food, will be to make arrangements by which the supply shall be continued. 

This must be done either by the actual gradual collection of depots, or by engageiments that 
may be relied on for supplies. 

25. The food which is most economical and nourishing, is soup made after any of the known 
receipts, together with a portion of bread, meal, or biscuit. 

26. One ration may consist either— 

Of 14 Ib. of bread, 

Or1_ |b. of biscuit, 

Or 1. 1b. of meal or flour of any grain, 

Or 1 quart of soup thickened with a portion of meal, according to the known receipts, 
and one quarter ration of bread, biscuit or meal in addition, 

Persons above nine years of age to have one ration, under nine, a half ration, in the day. In 
all cases, where practicable, the food should be given out in a cooked form, and should be 
varied oecasionally from bread rations to soup and bread, or meal and rice porridge with 
bread. 

27. The distribution of food to a large number of people is attended with many difficulties, 
and these are somewhat increased when soup is substituted for dry food, still the soup is so 
advantageous that it should be adopted wherever it may be at all possible. 

28. No person can on any account whatever be permitted to receive food gratuitously, unless 
named in the list of destitute persons. ; 

29. No rations are to be advanced in anticipation, except to the helpless poor, and the 
advance must not extend beyond the fortnight for which the relief has been allowed in each 
particular case. 

30. In order to husband the resources of the Committees, the sales should be limited to 
those on a list of persons considered to have a claim, who will be allowed to purchase the 
number of rations set against their names, which will be on the same scale, per individual of 
the family, as defined for gratuitous distribution, provided sufficient remains after the gratuitous 
relief of the day. 

31. The articles of food for sale should be also of the same nature as what are given free. 

32. The cost of hiring a clerk to conduct the business of the sale, and of his assistants, should 
be considered as part of the outlay which is to be covered by the price charged for each ration. 

33. No sale, except of soup, should be made at less than cost price, including carriage, 
with other necessary charges of buying and selling.

struggling for precedence, and many detained for long. periods, perhaps during whole nights, 
the lists should be made out numerically, in which order the persons should be called. A 
eard, printed in black, must be given to each person, who answers when called, with the 
number of rations to be delivered to him marked upon it, and the card is to -be given up on 
receiving the rations. A card, printed in red mk,-is to be used for the rations sold. The

42. All correspondence with the Commissioners must be carried on through the Finance 
Committee; and forms will be supplied, on which communications or inquiries are to be

Relief Committee, and that there is not included in it any sum contributed from 
funds applicable to charitable purposes, except as expressly set forth. 

45. In cases where any deviations from these Instructions may be considered advantageous, 
they may be proposed for the sanction of the Commissioners, on a full explanation of the 
circumstances. 

46. In conclusion, the Commissioners would urge upon the Committees, that the difficulties 
of meeting so extensive a calamity as has befallen this country are enormous ; and it is only 
by the most strenuous, uncompromising, and disinterested co-operation of every individual 
who has the power to contribute in funds or by personal exertions, that even a partial alle- 
viation can be afforded. It is hoped, therefore, that great efforts will be made to remove 
every impediment to the carrying out of the provisions of the Act of Parliament in the most 
effective manner; always bearing especially in mind, that since the funds that will be available 
for relief in each Union must be strictly limited to a certain definite sum, every farthing of 
money or atom of food applied unduly, would be so much abstracted from a famishing 
population.

Your first and immediate attention will be turned to the best means for collecting the

Government, by being quoted as emanating from its own officer. 

Various official documents will be in your hands, from which you will collect generally the 
intentions and principles adopted by Government, and the Relief Commissioners expect you will 
in all cases act up to them. 

In order to enforce on the minds of the public generally, and more particularly on the 
members of the Committees which shall be hereafter formed, the deep responsibility that 
attaches to them; you will cause to be constantly kept in view this essential difference in prin- 
ciple, that it is the Government is now coming forward to assist the local means and authorities, 
and not they assisting the Government. 

The Commissioners consider that you will haye it in your power to render far greater service 
in the execution of the charge entrusted to you, if you can gain the confidence of the 
Committees and people generally of the district, by adopting the most courteous and concili- 
ating diposition, while executing the duty with firmness, and by showing the most anxious desire 
to co-operate in the greatest degree in your power for the relief of the suffering population.

Tat Commissioners have received instructions from the Lords Commissioners of 
Her Majesty’s Treasury, directing that the following notice be immediately given :— 

“That from Saturday, the 20th March, the number of persons employed on the Relief 
Works will be reduced not less than 20 per cent., and that the remainder will be further 
diminished by successive reductions, in the proportions and at the times to be hereafter fixed, 
until a new system of Relief under the 10th Vict. cap. 7, shall have been brought into 
full operation.” 

For the purpose of carrying the above instructions into effect, you will make arrangements 
with the Relief Inspecting Officers of the Poor Law Union in which each work is situate, 

“ For the discharge on the 20th March of all persons at present destroyed holding 10 acres 
of land and upwar ds, even if they should exceed the proportion of 20 per cent. 

“Tf the number of such persons should not amount to the proportion above stated, those 
persons are to be discharged who hold the largest amount of land (although less than 10 acres), 
or of other property of any description.” 

And as at this season of the year, it is desirable that every inducement should be held out to 
labourers to return to the ordinary occupations of the country, or that those who cannot obtain 
labour should be relieved under the provisions of the 10th Vict., cap. 7, 

The Commissioners deem it necessary to discontinue the system of task-work, after the 20th 
instant, and employ labourers of every description, at a rate of daily wages, under that at 
present given by farmers in surrounding districts. 

You will enforce strict and constant attendance of the labourers from 6 A.M. to 6 P.M., 
by directing the overseer of each work to call the roll every morning at 6 o'clock, and from every 
person who does not attend at that hour, deduct a quarter of a day’s pay; from those who do not 
appear before 9 o'clock, deduct half a day’s pay. ‘Those who come after that hour will not be 
employed during the day. The roll will also be called at 6 p.m., and no payment for that day 
will be allowed to any person who does not appear. 

You will discharge all children under nine years of age, and all women who are not 
supporters of families. 

You will, as far as possible, discontinue the employment of all horses or asses,

Tur Relief Commissioners do not consider it advisable at present to add to Relief Com- 
mittees any persons not belonging to the classes described in the Lord Lieutenant’s Order of 
the 4th instant, therefore, the lists already transmitted will be reserved until a necessity may 
arise for submitting additional names for his Excellency’s appointment. 

The only step necessary before assembling Relief Committees, as constituted by the Order 
referred to, is, to determine the electoral divisions in which it may be requisite to carry the Act 
into effect ; and to obtain the Order of the Lord Lieutenant for that object. With this view, 
the Inspecting Officer should immediately consult the Finance Committee, and also make 
inquiries in the Union; reporting the opinion of the Committee, and his own, (founded on his 
inquiries), without delay. 

The attention of the Finance Committee, when considering this question, should be drawn to 
the discontinuance of grants on subscriptions collected by the old Relief Committees. Hence- 
forth, grants can only be made on subscriptions placed at the disposal of Committees consti- 
tuted according to the Lord Lieutenant’s Order of the 4th instant. 

Therefore, for the purpose of forming Relief Funds by subscriptions and grants in aid,—the 
new Relief Committees will be required, where the distress may not be so great as to render 
necessary a demand on the funds of the Board of Guardians, with a consequent resort to 
rates. 

In considering whether Relief Committees should be assembled for separate electoral diyi- 
sions, or combinations of them, it will be important to keep in view that when electoral divisions 
are united, every qualified person described in the Lord Lieutenant’s Order of the 4th instant, 
who may be connected with any of such divisions, as being within any of the classes specified 
in the Order, must be deemed a member of the Committee for the united divisions, and 
entitled to act on it at once. 

It is.also to be borne in mind that each of the divisions united in one Relief district, will, in 
case of requiring funds to be advanced on the security of poor-rates, be charged with the 
expenses of relief precisely as if each division were a separate Relief district. 

The clear understanding of these rules may remove objections to the junction of electoral 
divisions, when advisable, for forming Relief districts. 

Such junctions are, however, to be considered as exceptions to the general rule, and should 
not be made against the wishes of local parties interested in the several divisions, where sub- 
stantial objections are stated. 

In conclusion, it appears necessary to observe, (as information on the question has been 
sought), that a Relief Committee cannot be constituted for only a portion of an electoral divi- 
sion; and that portions of two or more divisions cannot be united to form a district.

7th April, 1847. 
Tue Relief Commissioners having received very numerous applications to alter the constitu- 
tion of Committees, think it right to explain their views on the subject more fully, which will, 


they trust, be a sufficient answer to many of the references made, and tend to prevent the 
transmission of others. 


I.—As to Finance Committees. 


The Finance Committees, by the Act, may consist of not less than two, nor more than four 
members, In order to afford a better chance of securing the habitual attendance of at least two 
gentlemen, the Commissioners have recommended four in almost every case. 

So long as there shall be two of the appointed list willing to act, a Finance Committee 
may be considered complete; and the Commissioners have not, in such cases, thought it neces- 
sary to take any immediate steps for the re-appointment of the entire number. 

It is not considered that an occasional or temporary absence for short periods would justify 
a member being accounted inefficient. 

In the selection of names to be recommended to the Lord Lieutenant for appointment, the 
object has not been to obtain a degree of representation for different Districts so much as to 
acquire the services of gentlemen having a general interest in the Union, of character and 
habits qualifying them efficiently to attend to their duties, and whose place of residence should 
be convenient to the place of meeting. 

The Commissioners, in these nominations, have not presumed to affix any superior merit or 
qualifications in those selected over many other gentlemen; in some instances, it is probable, 
a better choice might have been made, had the Commissioners been better informed, but in 
very few do they see reason to regret the appointments, on any other ground but that of the 
omission of some peculiarly eligible person. 

They do not, however, consider that they would be justified in recommending the Lord Lieu- 
tenant to supersede a member who has been once appointed, and has proved himself to be 
competent, and well qualified, solely to give place to another, however superior, 

‘There has been also an endeavour to obtain a mixture of creeds and parties among the 
members of each Committee; but in the present generally happy amalgamation of feeling 
for the public good, the Commissioners have not considered this a consideration worthy of 
attention, as compared with other merits or qualifications necessary for the fulfilment of the 
responsible and important duties to be performed.

II_—As to Relief Committees. 


In the constitution of Relief Committees, which have functions connected with considerable 
expenditure and taxation, it seemed very desirable to give them a fixed character of fair 
representation of different interests. 

With this view the Lord Lieutenant’s Regulation of the 4th March has been drawn up and 
made as comprehensive as possible, to endeavour to preclude in a great degree the necessity 
of individual nomination, which could not be entered into without danger of complaints, and 
of difference of opinion. 

Applications for specific additions to these Committees have been very general, on séveral 

leas, 
: Ist. Because there would be an actual want of qualified individuals coming under the classes 
designated in the Lord Lieutenant’s Regulation. 

2nd. Because there was a stfong anticipation that some members of Committees so consti- 
tuted would he negligent or incompetent. 

3rd. With a view of adding parties who were known to be zealous, active, and willing 
members of such associations, although there might be a sufficiency of competent members 
under the Regulation. 

The first plea, which formed the only complete objection, has been obviated in many 
instances by the union of two or more Electoral Divisions under one Committee; and it 
appeared so desirable to decline entering upon the consideration of a multiplicity of individual 
claims and representations, that the Commissioners have avoided recommending the Lord 
Lieutenant to adopt that discretionary power until there should be proof of its absolute neces- 
sity, in justification of a course that must be attended with great inconveniences. 

The services of these Committees are so essentially interwoyen with the welfare of the 
respective Local Committees, and are so connected with the just administration of relief to a 
suffering population, that the Commissioners would anxiously hope that no individual would 
decline or evade the fulfilment of every part of the duties which his station requires, or 
enables him to perform; and they consider that it will be the impression of every honorable 
man that any district will be disgraced of which it can be reported that it contains individuals 
qualified and enabled to act, yet refusing or neglecting to come forward in the present emer- 
gency. 

The Commissioners request that the Inspecting Officers will make these considerations 
known to all persons by whom changes in Committees may be proposed. 

By order of the Relief Commissioners,

Tue black-ink cards for gratuitous relief are packed in parcels of 200 for each number, 
viz.—, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. 

Each person must be called numerically from the Relief List, and on answering the number 
and name, a card, with the number of rations set against his or her name, is to be given, 
which will be delivered up at the kitchen or store on receiving the quantity. 

If more rations are to be delivered than the cards are numbered, an additional card is to be 
given; for instance, if 7 are due, give two cards—5 and 2; if 43, give two cards—3 and 4. 

The numbers should be kept separate, either in different boxes or in a tray with six separate 
places to hold them, for convenience of delivery. 

After the rations have been delivered for the day, the number is to be ascertained by 
counting the numbers marked on the cards; they should then be separated into the different 


boxes or trays ready for issue on the next day, and so on, day after day. ;

If a person does not answer when called, a mark in pencil is to be made against the name 
on the list, to be inquired into, and brought before the Committee. 

The red-ink cards are packed in parcels of 50 for each number; they are to be used when 
rations are sold, from List B, and the. total number, when collected at the kitchen or store, 
should agree with the total amount received upon the day’s sale.

No gratuitous relief will be given, unless the person in distress has applied to the clerk, ; 


and his case has been investigated and approved of by the Relief Committee. 

When so approved, the name of the party will be entered on the Relief List, a number will 
be placed against his or her name, and the names will be called in the order of the numbers. 

If the party answers the call in turn, and by the number, a card will be given, showing how 
many rations are to be served out; the card must be given up upon receiving them. 

Should any person attempt to obtain the rations belonging to another, both names will be 
struck off the list, if both are concerned in the fraud. 

Every able-bodied poor person must appear daily to answer his call, and if he does not 
attend, owing to his being employed, his name will be struck off the list ; for gratuitous relief, 
as a pauper, is not to be allowed to a person in employment. 

Each person must take a can, or some vessel sufficiently large, to contain the quantity of soup 
or meal to be supplied. 

Any person crowding or causing a disturbance, by not keeping in his or her place in proper 
turn, waiting to be called, according to their number on the list, will not be served till the
