MAY IT PLEASE YOUR LORDSHIPS, 


WE beg to submit our Fourth Monthly Report as Commissioners under the 
Temporary Relief Act. 


. We have .now 1,893 electoral divisions for relief under the operation of the 
Act, which are distributing 2,342,900 gratuitous rations per day, at an average cost 
of two pence per ration, including expenses, and 79,636 rations are sold. 


The falling prices of provisions, and the small profit required by the lower class 
of traders, have tended to keep down the necessity for much selling by Committees. 


Your Lordships will perceive a considerable increase in the distribution, occa- 
sioned partly by the additional districts, which although among the most suffering, 
have been now, for the first time, brought under the Act; partly from the 
withdrawal of the supplies which had been so largely contributed by charitable 
associations for the relief of a state of absolute starvation, against which a general 
provision now exists; and from the reductions in the public works; but chiefly 
from the pressure of distress which it is notorious always weighs heavily on the 
agricultural population of Ireland at this season of the year. 


From the commencement of August, however, we shall look forward to great 
reductions ; the harvest promises to be very abundant, and as the temporary relief 
was intended to provide for the diminution of food by the failure of the potato crop, 
the gradual collection of the agricultural produce will remove every justification 
for its continuance on any other plea. 


By an arrangement with the Commissary-General, we are clearing out the 
Government depots of provisions, by orders on them in lieu of so much money. 


These depots were established at an anxious period of a prospect of great defi- 
ciency of supplies, which no longer exists. 

The number of temporary Hospitals ordered to be established under the Act 
10 Vict. cap. 22, now amounts to 288. 


Wherever opened they are reported to have been highly beneficial, but we regret to 
learn that the necessity for them generally in the country, is far from being abated. 


We annex the usual Returns, and copies of Circular Orders that have been issued 
since our last Report, together with the Summary of the Account Branch. 


We have, &c.,

SUMMARY OF THE ACCOUNTANT'S DEPARTMENT. 


To the Relief Commissioners acting under Act 10 Vic., caps. 7 and 22. 
19th July, 1847. 


In submitting the present Financial State of the Commission and its Accountancy during 
the past month, I beg to lay before the Commissioners certain returns, showing, it is hoped, 
the maximum daily relief that the Commission may be called upon to render. 

It will be seen, upon reference to the annexed statement (А,) that 2,643,128 rations are being 
issued, equal to the support of 3,020,712 persons daily. : 

The fall, however, in the price of provisions, has reduced the average cost of the ration by 
one-fifth since my last month's summary,—viz., from 254. to 2d. 

In consequence of this reduction in the price of provisions, there is (notwithstanding the great 
increase of 291,028 persons since last month upon the relief lists), ап estimated diminution of 
about 30007. daily, in demands for loans and grants, the amount of expenditure now being at 
about the rate of 20,0001. a day. 

'The aggregate amount advanced up to the present time, to 1803 electoral divisions, whether 
by way of loan or grant, is 1,010,184/. 7s. 104, as shown in statement (С.) ў 

The sums shown in this statement, under the head of grants, are in aid of private subserip- 
tions only. The grants in aid of rates will be separated from the sums shown under the column 
of loans, on the termination of the advances, when the charge as loans against each electoral 
division will be relieved in the proportions agreed to by the Commissioners, and approved of 
by the Lords of the Treasury. 

The cost of the Government staff for superintending the relief and controlling the expenditure 
is 2% per cent. only, or sixpence in the pound. 

The amount advanced to the present time, for the support of 152 temporary fever hospitals, 
15 951527. 

Iu consequence of the opposition shown in some cases to the use of cooked food, and for 
excesses in the issue of rations, intimidation and other causes, reductions have been made, by 
direction of the Board, in the estimates of 92 electoral divisions. 

It appearing that the Relief Committees were not resorting to the Commissariat depots for 
the purchase of provisions, and the Commissary-General having stated that it was the desire 
of the Government that the depóts should be exhausted of the stock in hand, the annexed 
< Circular, No. 23, Finance,” was issued to the several Committees, and upon receipt in 
office of the estimates for the past fortnight, orders were issued upon the depóts for the supply of 
provisions in lieu of money to 933 electoral divisions, already amounting to 98,723/., being 
equivalent to about 8,000 tons. 

As this system will be continued for the ensuing fortnight, it is expected that most of the 
depóts will be exhausted by the first week in August. 

The prices charged by the Commissary-General are at the daily market price, which appears 
in some instances below the estimated price reported by the Committees. 

The value of the supply will be transferred weekly to the Commissary-General, and the 
respective electoral divisions charged with the same. 

The sensible advantage of securing by this means an immediate return to the Government 
of a very large sum of money by the sale of a stock of provisions at the market price, in lieu 
of submitting it to sale by auction, or of storing it with the chances of deterioration, was so 
apparent, that I did not hesitate for a moment to incur the risk of confusion in my accounts, and 
undertake the great increase of labour and responsibility of subdividing the numerous fort- 
nightly estimates, it being necessary to send a part in money as well as in provisions in each 
case. 

7,974 distinct sums have already been issued to the Relief Committees, which require about 
an equal number of accounts to be rendered. "To subdivide these sums, and increase the 
number of accounts to be rendered and examined, will give some idea of the increased labour 
to my department, which I need scarcely remind the Commissioners has been already over- 
worked. 

The Account Current and Balance Sheet, to 30th June last, of the general Cash trans- 
actions of the Commission, have been made up and tendered to the Auditors, the examination 
of which is being proceeded with, 

The Monthly Accounts of the several Treasurers to Finance Committees have been sent to 
the Auditors, together with some Electoral Divisions' Accounts, upon which they are calling 
daily for explanation. 

The majority of the latter accounts I regret to say are most unsatisfactorily rendered. 

I beg to annex the continuation ** Forms," and * Circulars 18 to 23 Finance,” referring to 
the details of my department. 


R. M. Вкомтку, 
Accountant to the Relief Commission.

cooking ?"— and in reply, the Board desire to observe, that the precise proportion for the object 
in view could not be fixed, as it would often necessarily vary according to circumstances of sea- 
sons, predisposition to disease, and even individual constitutions ; but that as a general rule, 
rice may be mixed with advantage in almost any proportions, however small, with Indian 
meal, and in larger proportions according as its cost may permit; care, however, being always 
taken, as explained in previous letters of the 11th May and Ist June (see Extracts annexed), 
that each ration when cooked should contain its full equivalent of raw meal and rice, as recom- 
mended by the Board of Health in the same communications. | 

Тће Board of Health, although admitting the advantage of bulk occasionally acquired in 
food by cooking, yet taking into consideration one of the forms of disease now prevalent in 
Ireland, do not recommend that rice should be used with the object of giving bulk by the ab- 
sorption of watery particles; but, on the contrary, that cooked rations should be given out in as 
solid a form as possible, leaving it to the natural feelings of thirst to supply the quantity of 
fluid necessary for healthy digestion. With the object also of obviating the general predisposi- 
tion to relaxation of the bowels, which will probably increase as the summer advances, the 
Board of Health recommend the addition of some of the ordinary condiments to the stirabout, 
of which, perhaps, pepper will be found the most acceptable and the cheapest. 

Тће Board of Health takes this opportunity of acquainting the Relief Commissioners, that 
they have learned through the Inspectors, and from other sources, that a diminution in the pre- 
valence of Diarrheea, and a marked improvement in the general health and strength of the 
people, have followed upon the substitution of cooked food for raw meal, and they, therefore, 
again recommend that every exertion should be made for extending the use of cooked food in 
the distribution of rations. 

While on the subject of diet, the Board of Health deem it of importance, through the me- 
dium of the Relief Commissioners, to draw the attention of Relief Committees, and of the people 
generally, to the fact, that a form of disease now very prevalent among the people, resembling 
in many particulars sea scurvy, is connected with, or dependent on a defective nutrition, arising, 
not from deficiency of quantity, but from deficiency of quality or variety in the food. The 
potato, although not containing a large proportion of nutriment, is remarkable as containing 
within itself all the varied elements necessary for forming healthy blood ; no one of the substi- 
tutes for it, with the exception of oatmeal, possesses this remarkable property ; and hence, the 
use of more than one article of food is necessary, in order to secure а sufficient supply of the 
varied elements of nutrition, and thus to obviate the form of disease referred to. 

With this principle in view, the Board of Health, as the season of the year now gives facili- 
ties for it, strongly recommend the admixture of onions, leeks, scallions, or shallots, in cooked 
meal rations, or stirabout, which will both act as condiments, and will also furnish some of the 
elements of nutrition essential for forming healthy blood. Onions are so rich in nutritive quali- 
ties, that as а mere article of food, one ounce and a half may be considered as equivalent to 
about one ounce of meal. On every opportunity, the importance to health of using vegetables 
uad carrots, parsnips, turnips, and cabbage along with meal, should be impressed upon the 
people. 

It may not, perhaps, be out of place in these observations to make the people generally 
acquainted with the high nutritive qualities of beans and peas, which, weight for weight, con- 
tain from 10 to 12 times as much nutriment as potatoes. In those countries where rice is very 
extensively used as the food of the people, experience has taught them that rice by itself has 
low nutritive qualities, but that the deficiency can be supplied by adding about two ounces of 
beans, peas, or lentils to each pound of rice. It must however be remembered, that with beans 
or peas, some other vegetable food must be used, as, although possessing in a high degree 
some of the most important elements of nutrition, they nevertheless do not contain a sufficient 
variety of other nutritive elements, to support the human body in health.

grain is given alone; and the same proportion should be observed in a mixed ration; viz., if 
half a pound of Indian corn meal be given instead of the standard ration of one pouud, there 
should be given along with it three-quarters of a pound of rice, to make the mixed ration equi- 
valent to one pound of Indian corn meal; and in like manner of all other proportions, in which 
rice and Indian corn meal mixed may form a ration. 

Тће Board of Health cannot hesitate to assure the Relief Commissioners, that rice instead 
of being superior, is much inferior to Indian corn meal in nutritious qualities. 

It is true, that rice by steeping and boiling, may be made to furnish a larger bulk of appa- 
rently solid food than will be furnished by an equal quantity of Indian corn, and hence has 
probably arisen the supposition of its being more nutritious; but the bulk thus obtained is 
deceptive, as to the quantity of nutriment afforded. Rice contains about 85 parts in every 100 
ofstarch. А given quantity of it will, by steeping and boiling, absorb a very large proportion 
of water, and will swell into a large and apparently firm mass; but this mass will contain a 
very small proportionate quantity of nutriment. А very simple experiment will satisfactorily

Tue Relief Commissioners receive numerous statements of the misappropriation of the 
means provided for the temporary Relief. 
Agricultural and other labour is said to be extensively carried on by hired men, women, and 
children, who are, at the same time, receiving gratuitous rations.

This kind of imposition is still more practicable in cases of mechanics in towns; and unfortu- 
nately it is most easy, where the Committees have made the best arrangements for ezpediting

While these circumstances are reported to the Commissioners in a manner that will not 
admit of doubt, they find there isa very general reluctance to denounce the facts openly, arising, 
in some instances, from the fear of drawing on the informant revengeful acts of violence, against 
person or property ; in others, from a false principle of stigma attached to information given 
even on such matters of fraud. 

There is also much reason to believe that the object of the Relief Act is greatly perverted, 
and that it is frequently applied, solely as means of adding to the comforts of the lower classes, 
and of assisting the farmers and employers in carrying on their business, instead of being, as 
intended, a provision exclusively for the utterly destitute, and for the purpose of warding off 
absolute starvation. 

If the Commissioners had not distinct testimony of these serious evils, in addition to the more 
gross abuses that are practised in some quarters, they must be impressed with a conviction of 
their existence, from the enormous demands on the Relief Funds, which manifestly must greatly 
exceed the real wants of the country. 

The present relief issues under the Act 10 Vict. сар. 7, must draw to a close shortly, and it 
is very necessary that determined steps should be taken for reducing them previously, by every 
opportunity that will be afforded by the employment of the approaching season, and by taking 
a more correct view of the application of the measure. 

The calamitous state of Ireland was considered a justification for providing food for the mul- 
titude, but the profuse manner in which it is in very many quarters administered, if allowed to 
continue unchecked, will prove of serious consequence to the whole social system of the 
country. 

The Inspecting Officers are requested to bring these matters under the serious consideration of 
the Committees, in order to obtain their active co-operation in limiting, as is most necessary, 
the present enormous expenditure, and causingit to be reduced as the season advances, to a 
2914 that will prevent the inconvenience of an abrupt termination to so extended a system of 

elief. 

It is clear that the Committees, the ratepayers, and persons of good feeling, can alone effect 
the desired object, on a system of perfect equity to all classes. 

The Inspecting Officer will afford them every aid, or, if obliged to act independently, will use 
every possible exertion to place the relief on a proper footing, and will report from time to time 
to what extent he may succeed, and what is the general state of his Union in this respect. 

It must be borne in mind that relief is not due to those who are in absolute want, unless they 
are unavoidably so; and any man who can if he pleases, obtain work at even moderate wages, 
should be refused gratuitous Rations. 

By order of the Relief Commissioners,

Tue Relief Commissioners have to recur to the subject of their Circular (No. 60), of 
6th July, as a matter of the deepest importance to the country. 

The present relief measure must close entirely before long, and much distress and confusion 
will arise unless the greatest efforts are made, by timely preparation, to provide by the means 
that will then exist for the support of the people now receiving rations. 

The best arrangement which can possibly be made for this purpose, will be by the remune- 
rative labour that ought to be arising at this period, and the most scrupulous attention to 
making it the means of an immediate corresponding reduction in the distribution of gratuitous 
relief. 

The Commissioners have great reason to believe that this is not effected in many places ; 
and that many men are engaged for others in hay-making, turf-cutting, and various works, 
without being withdrawn, as they ought to be, as well as their families, from the Rations’ List. 

This is partly facilitated, as the Commissioners are informed, by the attendance of able- 
bodied labourers at the daily distribution of rations, being in some places dispensed with on 
slight excuses. 

Very determined measures should now be taken to relieve the lists of labourers and their 
families, where work is to be had, even at moderate wages, and which is sometimes refused. 

A very active and zealous officer, has obtained a great, and clearly, a proper reduction in 
the lists, by organizing a test of useful work, of a few hours daily duration. He states— 

«І have got implements from the Public Works Department, for which the Committees 
become responsible, and make the able-bodied work in gangs for three or four hours daily, 
cleansing and sewering the town, sinking pumps, &c.; also stone-breaking, the broken stone 
being sold and the proceeds placed to the credit of the Committees. 

“І am adopting this system generally, and find that it clears the lists of all doubtful cases, 
and is attended with the best effects.” 

The Commissioners cannot but complain of finding the demands for rations from many dis- 
tricts continually increasing, and sometimes largely, without even a word of explanation to 
account for it; as if whatever was asked for, was at once allowed.
