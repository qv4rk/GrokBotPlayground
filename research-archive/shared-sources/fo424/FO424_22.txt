The deep importance of the subject to which they refer, entailing the

ment of native agriculture and industry by a remodelling of the fiscal system,

been effected, nor can anything be done towards their introduction until the 
Central Government takes the initiative.

Of many of the laws the people are kept in total ignorance, Were they

on account of the irregular mode of their election. These are the members who 
frequently exercise a most baneful influence over the President by encouraging the 
continuance of old abuses, which it is their interest to foster, and through which 
they often amass immense wealth. In some provinces members of this description 
gain such an ascendancy over the Pasha that they virtually rule the Pashalic. The 
ill-cotten wealth of these petty tyrants enables them to fallow with impunity their 
iniquitous course, for they can afford to dispense bribes in high quarters, so 
that oppression triumphs, and the cries of the suffering poor seldom reach the 
ears of their beneficent Monarch. It appears to me, therefore, that unless the 
present defects in the Medjlis, chiefly as regards the elections of the members, be 
eradicated, that institution will continue to be totally inefficacious to produce 
progress and good government. As so much depends upon the manner in 
whieh the chief functionaries choose to wield their authority, and on their 
‘ndividual bent of mind, the Porte ought to be cireumspect in the selection of 
its employes. It is notorious that many of those officials whose misconduct has 
entailed upon them the loss of office are subsequently re-admitted into favour 
and entrusted with other responsible posts, It were needless to add that the 
bad example thus set by the Porte cannot fil to foster laxness of principles 
amongst all classes of its employes.

Under the Medjlis system, the only remedy open to suitors is to have their

although L strongly condemn the conduct of those Who grant his as eb

own subjects, since the latter attach so much value to the possession of a 
foreign passport. If the Porte were to inquire into the motive by which their 
subjects are actuated, it would find that it proceeded entirely from the short- 
comings of the Turkish laws, and from their want of confidence in the justice of 
their rulers. ‘Their object is mainly to obtain security for their personal liberty 
and property against the frequent vexatious and arbitrary proceedings of Turkish 

officials, which is at once afforded them by the acquisition of a foreign passport. 

The imposstbility of obtaining speedy redress in case of complaint, and the 

implicit obedience required of all classes by the Government functionaries, who 
act capriciously, and give no explanation as to their motives, furnish fresh 
reasons for the frequent occurrence of this practice, It is the Porte, therefore. 
which could best prevent its subjects withdrawing themselves from its allegiance 
by shielding them against attacks on their liberty and property. 1 will here cite 
a fact in illustration of this subject:—A merchant resident at Smyrna, under 
Russian protection, shortly after the commencement of the late war, having been 
offered by the authorities the alternative of quitting the country, or of renouncing 
his foreign protection, aecepted the latter, and became an Ottoman subject. He 
soon after had great reason to repent of this step, for, at the instigation of the 
authorities, claims which had been settled in former years were brought against 
him by ‘Turks and by others, and he was condemned to pay them, even though 
In some cases the claims bad been previously examined and rejected, An 
attempt was moreover made to deprive him of a part of his landed property on 
frivolous pretexts; besides which, he was kept waiting for several hours without 
any ostensible motive. In fact, he states that his becoming a subject of the 
Porte seemed to be the signal for the authorities to commence persecuting him 
and compassing his ruin, 

It is not, therefore, a matter for surprise in the face of similar facts, whieh 
are of daily occurrence, that the Rayahs should occasionally resort to the only 
means at hand of protecting themselves against the shortcomings of their 
legitimate rulers; and it is the greatest reproach upon the Turkish Government, 
as Well as one of the most incontestable proofs of its present weak and degenerate 
state, that its own subjects should be compelled in sclf.defence to throw off their 
lawful allegiance, inasmuch as they are denied the protection which they have a 
right to expect, and are less favoured in this respect than foreigners: bein the 
reverse of what occurs in civilized countries. r 

When a foreign passport cannot be procured, the Rayahs find it adyan- 
tageous to carry on business in ostensible partnership with a foreigner or under 
a foreigner’s power of attorney. ‘This affords a great security for their property, 
and has become a common practice. 

This anomalous state of things will not cease to exist until the Porte has 
completed the task of reforming the present defective administration of justice, 
and has provided for that purpose properly-constituted tribunals. When that 
time arrives, the Ravahs will, { doubt not, cheerfully return to their allegiance, 
and the foreign Powers willingly relinquish the privileges of distinct jurisdiction 
aceorded by the Capitulations; but I may here add, that if those privileges were 
surrendered before any guarantee were afforded by Turkish institutions, no 
European would remain in the country,

WHEN at Nish Thad the honour of being received several times by the 
Grand Vizier, and I was also in frequent communication with the principal 
members of his Staff, Christian and Turks, as well as with the Governor-General, 
Qsinan Pasha; I, therefore, did not fail to seize the opportunities which my 
relations with these officials placed within my reach, to procure from them 
Information respecting the proceedings of the Mission in the Province of Nish; 
and I now beg to place before your Excellency all have learnt in connection 
therewith, 

I must first state that the Grand Vizier and all his subordinates told me 
more than once that the administration of this province was worse than they 
had reason to think ; and that in consequence they were forced to prolong their 
stay there much longer than they had anticipated. 

The Sadrazam remained about ten weeks in the province; he stopped a 
week at Berkoltaa, a town contiguous to the district of Widdin; three weeks 
at Sciarkiol, between Sofia and Nish; and forty-two days at the latter town. 

About 4,000 “arsuhals” (petitions) were addressed to his Highness by 
different partics in the province: several, many of them, carried frivolous com- 
plaints; and in some cases false charges, of a most serious nature, were advanced 
by the Bulgarian peasants against the native Beys, varying in nature and gravity, 
from the robbery of a goose ten years ago, to the ravishment of a young 
Christian virgin. 

The false accusers, in the latter instance, confessed that they had been 
excited by designing parties to make this charge. ‘They were sentenced to three 
years’ incarceration. 

The Grand Vizier, on his arrival in the Province of Nish, formed a tem- 
porary Council, before which all the most important and pressing cases were 
laid for settlement. Cuses of less moment were referred to the Local Courts. 

This Council had also to investigate the charges against the provincial 
officials, It was composed of the following Members of the Mission of his 
Highness :—M. Mussurus, M. Fotiades, M. A. Dadian, and M. Gabriel 
(Christian) ; Bessim Bey, Affi! Bey, Djeodjet Effendi (Turks). 

I] have been told that the decisions of this Council were in most cases 
carried by vote; that his Highness, although he had no vote, was very often 
present at its deliberations; and that every sentence it issued could not be 
executed unless previously confirmed by him. 

A Muvakat Medjlis (a Criminal Court, pro tem.), was also formed by the 
Vizier at Nish, and was composed of two Christians, two Turks, and one Jew, 
natives of the place, under Ae presidency of a certain Suleiman Bey, formerly 
Secretary of Legation at Vienna, All criminal cases were referred to this 
Court, and its sentences, if confirmed by the Grand Vizier, were immediately 
executed, 

T accidentally had an opportunity of watching the proceedings of this 
Court, and Tam glad in being able to report my having observed that the 
Christian members as well as the Jew were treated with the same regard as 
their Turkish colleagues, and that they took a prominent part in the delibera- 
tions. 

The establishment of this Court will be the source of much social good: 
wherever it has been instituted, in Thessaly, Epirus, Salonica, and Monastir, 
crime (brigandage in particular) hus abated, 

Measures lave also been taken by his Highness to secure the necessary 
firman frum the Sultan for the construction of a commercial tribunal at Nish, 
and Jam told that this instrument is daily expected. 

The Grand Vizier having found that most of the members of the Medjlises 
of Berkottza, Sciarkioi, and Nish, had acted with great negligence in the 
discharge of their duties, and were totally unfit for the position they occupied, 
forthwith dismissed them, and appointed others in their place. The new 
members were elected by the inhabitants of these towns, and their appointment 
ippears to have given satisfaction to the Christians and Turks,

(Extract.) Monastir, September 27, 1860. 

I HAVE the honour to report to your Excellency that his Highness the 
Grand Vizier, Mehemed Kiprisli Pasha, made his entry into Monastir on the 25th 
instant, arriving from Uscup, Kinpruli, and Perlépé. The whole town went out 
to welcome his Highness, whose reception was a most brilliant one. 

Accompanied by my colleagues the Vice-Consuls of Austria and Greece, | 
went, in plain clothes, to the spot outside the town where tents had been 
prepared for the reception of his Highness and his numerous suite. After 
receiving in his tent the Turkish civil and military officers, his Highness 
expressed a wish to see the Consuls, and he received us most courteously. I was 
given to understand subsequently that the Grand Vizier felt gratified at the 
unofficial manner in which the Consuls had greeted him, 

Since his arrival, the Grand Vizier has been occupied with inquiries into the 
general condition of this province. Yesterday he summoned all the members of 
the Grand Council, and the Primates of the Mussulman, Christian, and Jewislr 
communities, and undeceived them as to the truthof the rumour which had been 
circulated in the town to the effect that he intended to stay at Monastir only 
three days. His object was, he suid, to remain here sufficient time to enable him 
to ascertain what, if any, were the causes of complaint on the part of the popula- 
tion. His Highness added, that he had heard that, previous to his arrival, the 
public Scribes of the town had been instructed to abstain from writing any 
petitions to his address; and he hoped this was untrue, because he had wished 
it to be known that he was ready to receive the just complaints of all who had 
any to profier, and that he would act upon them. Since this manifestation of his 
Highness'’s wishes, petitions have been freely given in; but the subjects to which 
they refer are not yet known to me. Although I have been informed that a list 
of the names of cighteen persons connected with the administration of this 
wrovince, and suspected of illegal proceedings, has been handed in to the Grand 
Vizier, there is no outward appearance as yet of the conduct of any one of 
these having become the object of a formal investigation. It would seem as 
though his Highness were desirous of collecting as much information as lies 
in his power before prosecuting them. 

The very moment of his arrival, the Grand Vizier directed one of the 
gentlemen attached to his Mission to obtain a Return of all persons actually 
suffering imprisonment at Monastir, showing every particular as to the cause 
for which, and the article of the Criminal Code by which, they have been 
condemued., ' 

Yesterday I had the honour of waiting upon his Highness, who treated me 
with marked affability. He kept me more than half-an-hour in conversation, iti 
the course of which he touched upon the object of his Mission, and summed up, 
under four heads, the subjects with reference to which he contemplated, on his 
return to the capital, taking remedial measures, These were :— 

1, The mode of collecting the tithes ; 

2, The police system ; 

3. ‘The abuses of the Christian clergy ; 

4. The attributes of the Local Councils, 

With respect to the first point, his Highness objected to the system of farming 
out the tithes to speculators, 

As to the second, he observed that the police were too much underpaid for 
either honesty or zeal to beexpected from them, __ 

With reference to the third point, the rapacity and petty tyranny of the 
bishops, of which, during his tour, he had had ample evidence, were greater and 
more keenly felt by the Christian population than any which they suffered at the 
hands of the Mussulmans. 

On the subject of the Local Councils, the fourth point, the Grand Vizier 
was of opinion that they should be merely an administrative body, divested of all 
judicial functions.

Protest. 
(Translation from the Greek.) 


TASCO, the servant of Filio the carpenter, was, about a year ago, cast into 
prison, accused as the murderer of a certain young Turk, although it was he 
(the Turk), with others his companions, who hid attempted to assassinate a 
certain neighbour and friend of Tasca, 

In the month of May of the present year, the population having been 
informed that Taseo had been condemned to death in an underhand and informal 
manner, held a meeting and demanded by petitions addressed to the Governor 
Abdi Pasha, and to the Consuls, the revision of the trial, they being convinced of 
the innocence of young Tasco. 

It is now rumoured that a decision for his decapitation will be put into 
execution by order of the Grand Vizier. 

The Christian population of Monastir therefore protest against this act.
