My Lord, Constantinople, June 1, 1948. 

[HAVE reason in some respects to complain of the obstinate dis- 
position manifested by the Turkish authorities to carry out their own 
construction of the Commercial Treaty without waiting for the decision 
of Her Majesty’s Government on the points submitted to your Lordship’s 
consideration after my late discussions with Sarim. Effendi. [In lending 
every facility to the examination and adjustment of questionable or 
unsatisfactory clauses in the Treaty, | have never ceased to insist, as 
occasion required, upon the complete execution of the existing: engage- 
ments. Among the complaints which have reached me from the pro- 
vinces, and dating some time back, is the exaction of a road charge in 
certain mountain passes, called Derbends, in Europe and Asia. This 
Imposition is not of recent origin, but Lam assured that even prior to the 
conclusion of the present Commercial Treaty it was suecessfully resisted 
by Her Majesty's Consuls, and though a certain amount was occasionally 
levied, it was returned to the complaining parties on their demand, 

The French and American Legations have both remonstrated against 
the charge ia question upon the obvious ground of its being at variance 
with the express stipulation of the Fourth Article of the Convention, 

After various unprofitable endeavours to obtain from the Porte at 
least a temporary acquiescence in the qustice of my claim, | thought it 
advisable, in pursuance of your Lordship’s instructions concerning the 
salt sequestered at Salonica, to address an official note to Mountaz 
Effendi. A copy of this note is herewith inclosed, [ have not yet received 
ananswer, but nothing has transpired to encourage the expectation of 
my receiving a favourable one. 

It is my intention to treat the remaining: subjects of complaint in 
the same manner and upon the same principle. If I do not succeed in 
forcing the hands of the ‘Turkish Minister, [ shall at least improve my 
position on the receipt of your Lordship's instruction respecting: the 
disputed points of the Treaty, 

I should have presented my note on the subject of the Derbend 
charge at an earlier period if I had not been assured by Sarim Effendi 
that the Turkish mcabaueacee in London had received an intimation from 
your Lordship that the views of the Porte were favourably considered by 
ler Majesty’s Government, and that instructions corresponding with 

that idea would be speedily transmitted to me. 

From the Turkish Ministers, and also from the Russian Envo ; 
I learn that M. de Titow, on his return to Constantinople, will probably 
be empowered to negotiate a Commercial Convention on the basis of that 
concluded at Balta-Liman, he adoption of such a measure by the 
Court of St. Petersburgh would be very acceptable to the merchants of 
all nations trading under the provisions of the British Treaty, for although 
the Porte appears to have enforced in some parts of the empire its reso- 
lution of levying the additional duties upon the native buyer and seller, 
as the case may be, the facilities of evasion are so considerable that our 
merchants continue to carry on their trade at a serious disadvantage,

Constantinople, May 27, 1843. 

THE Undersigned, Her Britannic Majesty’s Ambassador Extraor- 
dinary and Plenipotentiary, has already on several occasions, and during 
a lapse of seyeral months, solicited the attention of his Excellency the 
Minister for Foreign Affairs to the Derbend charges levied on British 
merchandize. It is with much regret that he has hitherto found his 
representations upon that subject unavailing, and it is now become his 
indispensable duty to press the matter still more earnestly upon the 
consideration of the Sublime Porte, 

The charges in question are a clear undeniable infraction of the Con- 
vention of Balta-Liman. They are contrary to the spirit of that Treaty, 
which substituted a large increased export duty of fixed amount in lieu 
of the vexatious undefined imposts previously levied; and they are con- 
trary to its letter, inasmuch as the Fourth Article expressly declares 
that no duty, or charge of any kind whatever, shall be levied on articles 
of Turkish produce or manufacture purchased for exportation by British 
merchants on their way to a convenient place for shipment. 

It is not enough for the Ottoman Ministers to allege that the charge 
shall be light, and the excess restored. The charge is altogether wrong 
in principle. Lt is incompatible with the rights secured to Her Majesty's 
subjects by treaty; and if there be one thing which concerns more 
deeply than another the honour and interests of the Porte, it is the con- 
tinuance of her ancient strict regard for treaty engagements, 

Still less is it open to the Ottoman Ministers to found any claim of 
exception on the confidential communications which have taken place 
between them and the Undersigned with a view to the introduetion of 
certain amendments in the Commercial Convention. The Undersigned 
declared from the outset that whatever modifications might eventually 
be admitted into the Treaty by mutual consent, the strict execution of 
the existing provisions was to be faithfully maintained during the interval. 
Her Majesty’s Government have given him positive and peremptory 
instructions upon this point, and the Undersigned, who has been happy 
to convey the wishes of the Porte to the favourable consideration of his 
Government, cannot but apprehend the effect of an obstinate resistance to 
his just demands for the execution of the Treaty as it now subsists. 

The ease, if it were a doubtful one, might admit of a dilferent view. 
But the terms of the Treaty are so clear that not a shadow of uncertainty 
rests upon them; the word “charge” appears to have been added to that 
of “duty,” in the Article already cited, for the express purpose of 
removing the possibility of doubt or eavil, 

Even before the conclusion of the present Convention the payment of 
the Derbend charges was resisted, and the Undersigned is warranted, by 
the reports of the British Consuls, in stating that in virtue of the ancient 
Capitulations also restitution was ordered by the Porte, when payment 
had been exacted on the spot. 

It may justly be added that the Porte, in admitting that the charge 
has been carried to a vexatious extent, and promising redress in that 
respect, gives evidence of the necessity which existed for extinguishing, 
by the special provisions of the Treaty, every kind of charge beyond the 
3 and 9 per cent, export duties, for the last of which the Porte is indebted 
to the free and friendly acquiescence of the British Government. 

Those parts of the Convention which have been brought forward 
with a view to modification are now before the British Cabinet, and they 
will, na doubt, be considered ina friendly and equitable spirit; but the 
discussions which preceded their transmission to England, were only 
directed to a clearer definition and understanding of their respective 
bearings; nor can the Undersigned admit that he has contracted any 
engagement or warranted any claim which supersede the parenonns 
obligation of carrying into full effect the Treaty as it still subsists, and 
as it was in reality administered for more than two years after its 
ratification.

THE early departure of the mail of the 6th instant prevented me from 
doing more by that opportunity than merely to communicate to your 
Excellency the instructions, of which | had just received a copy from St. 
Petersburgh, that the Russian Government had addressed to their 
Minister at the Porte, authorizing him to enter into a discussion on coms 
mercial matters with the Turkish Ministers. 

Since the date of my despatch No. 34, Her Majesty's Government 
have attentively considered the instructions to M. de Titoff, and although 
they feel that those instructions are not sufficiently explicit to enable them 
to arrive at any certain conclusion as to the general character of the new 
Convention of Commerce which Russia is about to negotiate with 
Turkey, they are still gratified to find that the instructions clearly 
state that the Russian Government are prepared to admit as 
one of the bases of the negotiation, the rates of supplemental, or 
commutation, duties, such as they are fixed by the Convention between 
Great Britain and Turkey of August 1838. Her Majesty’s Government 
therefore trust that they are warranted in hoping that the grievance 
under which the commerce of England and of other countries at present 
labours on account of the preference enjoyed by Russian subjects in this 
respect is about to be removed, 

At the same time, however, Her Majesty’s Government feel that the 
grievance to which I have referred has been so long endured, and is so 
serious in its character, that it is their duty to prevent, as far as possible, 
any delay in the negotiations between Turkey and Russia, which should 
tend unnecessarily to prolong the existence of this grievance; and they 
therefore wish that your Excellency should avail yourself of every suitable 
opportunity to counteract any tendency to procrastinate on the part of

Inclosure in No. 9. 
Mr. Mac Gregor to Mr. Addington, 


Office of Committee of Privy Council for Trade, 
Sir, Whitehall, December 20, 1843. 

[ AM directed by the Lords of the Committee of Privy Council for 
Trade to acknowledge the receipt of your various communications, with 
their inclosures, relative to certain points connected with the Commercial 
Convention with Turkey of August, 1538. 

My Lords are of opinion, upon a review of these papers, and of the 
Treaty to which they refer, that the question of the right of Great Britain 
is, in this case, a clear one, while that of the policy which it may be 
expedient to pursue is somewhat perplexed. 


The points raised are these :— 


First. Whether the British Government is entitled to demand from 
Turkey that the subjects of this country shall pay no greater an amount 
than 3 per cent. of import and export duty, instead of 5 and 12 per cent. 
respectively, the amounts stipulated for a specific equivalent in the 
Treaty, on the ground that the subjects of Russia pay no more than the 
smallest of these sums, 


Secondly. Whether, if this claim be a valid one, it is nevertheless a 
legitimate consequence of its being acknowledged that Turkey will be set 
at liberty to reconstitute the system of permits, and to re-establish 
monopolies. 


Thirdly. Whether it would, in like manner, follow that Turkey 
would, under the circumstances supposed, be free to re-impose internal 
duties to such amount as she might think fit. 


Fourthly. Whether British subjects are entitled, under the Treaty, to 
engage in retail trade within the Ottoman dominions. 


Upon this last question my Lords are of opinion that the claim is 
good, whether the words omitted in the Turkish version be taken into 
view or net; and that this claim should be immediately and uneondi- 
tionally urged upon the Turkish Government. 

Having thus disposed of this separate and simpler point, my Lords 
revert to the preceding questions. 

With regard to the first, they are clearly of opinion that Great 
Britain is entitled, under the First Article of the Treaty, to claim for her 
subjects exemption from the 9 per cent. and the 2 per cent. now levied in 
lieu of internal duties, upon the ground that the subjects of Russia enjoy 
that exemption. 

My Lords consider, with respect to the second question, that the 
abolition of permits and of monopolies, under the Second Article, is 
GRCORGIGAEL and that the right to establish them would not revive in 
consequence of the recognition of the claim for exemption to which I last 
adverted, 

But with regard to the third question, it appears undeniable that 
inasmuch as the stipulated payments of 9 and of 2 per cent. on exports 
and imports respectively, are expressly declared to stand in lieu of all 
interior duties, the existence of those payments is the only bar to such 
interior duties, and consequently upon the abrogation of them the dis- 
eretionary power to impose afresh such interior duties, and that without 
any limit of quantity, will revive. 

The questions of right then, in the view of my Lords, are clear: but 
the question of prudence which arises is, whether it be advisable to pursue 
the claim for a relief from the certain and comparatively moderate pay- 
ments of 9 and 2 per cent,, at the risk of the re-imposition of interior 
duties, which may be alike oppressive from their amount and from their 
uncertainty. 


16 


The substance of the inconvenience at present experienced is, that 
inasmuch as the subjects of Russia, including, of course, all persons who 
may hereafter acquire that title, are and will be entitled to import and 
export at 3 per cent.; and, as it Bpbente) that in practice the goods 
imported by them are not liable to the peyment of interior duties ; im 
addition, they enjoy the exemption purchased by us for an equivalent, 
without being liable to render that equivalent; anda direct premium of 
9 per cent. for export, and 2 per cent. for import, is placed upon the trade 
of Russian subjects, as compared with that of the subjects of other 
countries. 

The inconvenience to be apprehended in the event of the recognition 
of our claim to be treated on the footing of the most favoured nation ts, 
that one of the main benefits secured by the Treaty of 1868 would be lost 
by the restoration of internal duties, and a very serious impediment might 
be placed in the way of commerce with Turkey. 

But the burden in the latter case would equally menace all countries, 
and all countries would have a common interest in averting tts re-imposi- 
tion, or procuring its removal; and in the case before them, as my Lords 
apprehend, the union of all countries would substantially tnsure the attain- 
ment of the object upon which they were agreed, 

The inconvenience which actually subsists may be absolutely less, 
but relatively it is greater, and the established operation of such a 
premium must gradually divert to the Russian Flag all, or the greater 
part of, the trade now carried on under that of England, with the further 
evil of a deeper cast, that it powerfully tends to encourage fraud and 
collusion. 

My Lords consider that it cannot fairly be expected of British traders 
that they should contentedly acquiesce in the disadvantages of a position 
which forces them to compete, under the fine or penalty of a differential 
duty, not merely with some other nation of an inferior commercial develope-

THE Undersigned, Her Britannic Majesty's Ambassador Extraor- 
dinary and Plenipotentiary, has frequently applied to the Sublime Porte 
on behalf of certain British merchants, who have complained of restrictions 
and charges imposed upon their trade by the local Turkish authorities 
in violation of the existing Commercial Treaty. In one instance two 
cargoes of salt belonging to an English merchant at Smyrna were 
forbidden not only to be sold, but even, for many months, to be landed. 
In another ease the sale of a quantity of alum was in like manner pro- 
hibited, A similar prohibition NEE operated against the sale of tobacco 
or snuff, and some other articles of trade. In two instances the shops 
where snuff was offered for sale, have been arbitrarily closed by the local 
authorities. The charge levied upon merchandize at the Derbends, or 
mountain passes, whether in Asia or in Europe, is another object of 
serious complaint, affecting prejudicially and unjustly the interests of the 
British trader. 

The Undersigned observes with much concern that his repeated 
representations upon these several points have hitherto been attended 
witha very limited and unsatisfactory effect. 

He has, nevertheless, proved beyond adoubt that the above mentioned 
restrictions and charges are contrary to the express terms of the Treaty 
of Balta-Liman, He has made known to the Turkish Ministers, in detail, 
the losses incurred by those individuals, from whom the benefits of the 
Commercial Convention have been withheld. He has caused the state- 
ments and protests of the said merchants to he submitted to the same 
Ministers. He has not concealed from his Excellency the Minister for 
Foreign Affairs that Her Majesty’s Government expect these exceptions 
to the fulfilment of the commercial stipulations to be removed without 
further delay. 

The immediate objects of the Treaty concluded at Balta-Liman were 
the cessation of all undue exactions and arbitrary restrictions, the aboli- 
tion of monopolies, and the admission of British merchants to the internal 
trade of the country. These advantages were purchased by a large 
increase of the regular import and export duties, a proportional burden 
being thereby laid upon British trade, and a proportional benefit aceruing 
therefrom to the Turkish revenue. It is aealaie by the Seeond Article of 
the Treaty, that “the Sublime Porte formally engages to abolish all mono- 
solies of agricultural produce, or of any other article whatsoever." In the 
‘fourth Article, it is stipulated that goods purchased by the British 
merchant or his agent, even when produced, grown, or manufactured in 
Turkey, shall pe to the outports, free of any kind of charge or duty 
whatsoever, Y the Second and Third Articles a full participation in the 
internal trade of the Turkish Empire is secured to the British merchant; 
and by the former of those Articles all Turkish functionaries are held 
responsible for any infraction of the Treaty, and bound to indemnity the 
sufferer from any proved losses consequent thereon. 

Now, in the restrictions imposed on the sale of alum, salt, and snuff, 
the abolished principle of monopoly is revived; by the exclusion of British 
traders from the traffic in snuff their right of internal trade is violated; 
by the exaction of Derbend dues, their right to be free from any char 
but the 12 and the 5 per cent. duties is virtually annulled. The Bora by 
sanctioning such abuses, violates the very character and intention of the 
Treaty, which, instead of compensating the supplementary duties, is thus, 


in several important respects, turned against the very interests which the 
Treaty was meant to secure, and perverted to the confirmation of princi- 
ples which it engages to abolish. 

No excuse for the contempt and violation of positive engagements is 
to be found in pleading that the monopolies of salt, alum, and tobacco, 
were of a peculiar character; that ue Derbend dues are not charged upon

Mr. Gladstone to M. de Hummelauer. 


Mr. W. E. GLADSTONE begs leave to acknowledge the receipt of 
M. de Hummelauer's note of the Ist of April, and of the confidential 
memorandum inclosed in it; and he proceeds to reply, on the part of Her 
Majesty’s Government, to the questions proposed in it. 

M. de Hummelauer has made the confidential enquiry, whether the 
British Government would be disposed to assent to the reconsideration of the 
rates fixed by the Treaty of 1838, for the supplemental duties upon foods 
imported and exported, of 2 and 9 per cent. respectively, with a view to 
proposing to the Ottoman Government the substitution of other rates 
more adapted to the interests of that empire, and to the wants of parti- 
cular localities, that is to say, as appears from the earlier part of the 
memorandum, (confirmed and explained by the oral expressions of M. de 
Nummelauer,) of the Danubian provinces. 

As regards this branch of the question, the British Government are 
by no means disposed to press for the enforcement of the Treaty of 1838 
in the Danubian provinees, to the previously established circumstances of 
which it does not appear to be adapted. 

With regard to the alteration of the rates of 9 and 2 per cent., fixed 
by the Treaty of 1838, in lieu of all interior duties upon exports and imports 
respectively, Her Majesty's Government are prepared to express their 
concurrence in the principle that justice and sound rolicy demand the 
adoption of a somewhat different course in the case o Turkey from that 
which is conformable to the usual practice of European Powers among 
themselves, and that other States, in the regulation of their commercial 
arrangements with the Ottoman Empire, must assume, in a certain degree, 
a guardianship of its interests as well as of their own, And Her Majesty's 
Government entirely assent to the propositions, that the consi able 
charge laid upon exports is impolitic, and that it places the Turkish 
producer at a serious disadvantage with his competitors in the markets of 
the world; that the want of a coasting system, together with the levy of 
both export and import duties upon commodities carried by sea from one 
portion of the empire to another, is a grievous internal defect which ought, 

or the interests of the people, to be supplied; and lastly, that if the state 
of Turkish finance be such as to render it necessary that a larger revenue 
should be raised, it might even be right to entertain the question, presumin, 
other means not to be available, whether in addition to the total or partia 
commutation of duty on exports for duty on imports, the aggregate or 
standard rate of the tariff might not be somewhat increased, Her 
Majesty's Government would, indeed, on no account be parties to such a 
modification of the stipulations of the Treaty of 1838, as should have for its 
object the establishment of what is termed a protective system in Turkey, 
because they are not inclined to believe that such a course would be 
conducive to its interests; but they understand the proposal of M. de 
Hummelauer in a different sense, and they view it as confined to the 
purposes of removing the positive inequalities which the present Treaty, 
combined with the internal system, establishes to the prejudice of Turkish 


industry, and of introducing such other changes as each Power, in the 
exercise of its discretion, might judge to be demanded by a reasonable 
regard to the exigencies of Government in the empire.

(No. 101.) 
Sir, Foreign Office, July 2, 1844, 
HER Majesty’s Government are glad to perceive by your Excellency's 
despatches, Nos, 104 and 107, of the 2oth and 30th of May, that the Porte 
has abandoned its intention of prohibiting the exportation of grain, 

Her Majestv’s Government agree with your Excellency in considering 
that the plea adduced by the Turkish Government was not sufficiently 
valid to authorize them to adopt a measure which was decidedly in con- 
travention of the express terms of the Second Article of the Convention of 
1838; and it may be useful that your Excellency should state to the Porte 
that the inconsiderate manner in which the Porte attempted to prohibit 
the exportation of grain from Turkey in the present instance, in violation 
of the Treaty engagements subsisting between England and Turkey, has 
produced « painful impression on the minds of Her Majesty's Government, 
and might even cause them to doubt of the propriety of relaxing any of 
the stipulations of the Convention of Balta-Liman, however anxiously such 
a relaxation of them may be desired by the Porte. 

I am, &c.,
