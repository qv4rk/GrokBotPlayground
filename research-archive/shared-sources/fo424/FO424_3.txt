The Earl of Aberdeen to Sir Stratford Canning, 
(No. 158.) 


Sir, Foreign Office, October 7, 1844. 

IN my despateh No, 141 of the dthoflast month I transmitted to your 
Excellency some papers which had been communicated to me by the Aus- 
trian Minister at this Court, in further explanation of the views of the Cabinet 
of Vienna, as to the principles upon which the Powers of Europe interested 
in the welfare of Turkey should regulate their commercial intercourse with 
the Porte, 

The modification of the existing rates of commutation duties forms, 
as your Exeellency will have seen in these papers, as well as in those com- 
municated to you In my despatches No. 84 of the 5th of June, and No. 106 
of the Sth of July, a main feature of the Austrian plan; the Cabinet of 
Vienna conceiving that the present rates are calculated to give an unfair 
advantage to the foreign trader over the domestic producer, and thus 
lead eventually to the impoverishment and ruin of the Ottoman Kimpire, 
which it is the object of the Powers of Europe to uphold. 

Her Majesty’s Government, as your Excelleney is aware, coincide so 
far in the opinion of the Court of Vienna as to be willing to co-operate 
with other Powers in relieving Turkey from the disadvantage under which 
it is alleged to labour in this respect. Whether, indeed, the Porte is 
sensible of the extent of the disadvantage which the Cabinet of Vienna 
considers to result to it from the present system has not, so far as Her 
Majesty’s Government are aware, been made evident, On the contrary, 
from an observation in M. Hummelauer’s Memorandum of the Ist of 
April, it would rather appear that the Cabinet of Vienna is not without 
apprehension that the present Ministry of the Porte, with the usual short- 
sightedness which characterizes the councils of Turkey, might be disposed 
to demur to a change in the rates of commutation duties which, however 
pregnant with real benefit to the country at large, might involve in the 
first instance a diminution of the receipts of the Turkish treasury, M, 
Hummelauer says:— Les Ministres Turques ne voyent dans les Traités 
et dans les 'Tarifs, que des mesures fiscales, une machinerie destinée 
uniquement a faire entrer de Vargent dans les cuisses du Gouvernement; ce 
n'est done pas avec les Ministres ‘Turques qu'il serait utile d’ouvrir des 
pourparlers préalables,” &e. 

ie this as it may however, the British Government have the real and 
sermanent interests of ‘Turkey so much at heart, that they would not 
hesitate, even at some saerifice in the first instance of the interests of the 
British manufacturer, to co-operate with other Powers in endeavouring to 
persuade the Ottoman Ministers to take an enlarged view of what the 
welfare of Turkey requires. 

Her Majesty's Government moreover agree with the Cabinet of Vienna 
in considering that it is not incumbent upon them to wait, as the French 


2 


Government appear disposed to do, until the period arrives for renewing 
the Tariffs of 1839, namely, until the year 1846, before making the attempt 
to enter into a common understanding with the Porte as to the modifica- 
tion of the existing rates of commutation duties. At the same time how- 
ever, the experience which Her Majesty's Government have had of the 
serious inconvenience which results to British trade when one Power holds 
‘tself aloof from the commercial engagements subsisting between others 
and the Porte, would render it in fhiele opinion extremely injudicious to 
moot the question with the Porte, unless with the unanimous concurrence 
of all the Powers of Europe having treaties with Turkey. 

But while Her Majesty's Government concur so far with the Cabinet 
of Vienna, they cannot admit with that Cabinet the expediency of recom- 
mending the Russian Government to postpone its acc uiescence in the scale 
of commutation duties fixed by the Treaty of 1838, until a new and 
general arrangement of those duties shall have been discussed and 
adopted by all Powers and the Porte. On the contrary, Her Majesty's 
Government conceives that the evil which results from the undue advan- 
tage enjoyed by Russian over British trade should be recdressed 
without delay; and on account of the immediate and pressing nature 
of that evil, they would hesitate to participate in any attempt to 
discuss the general question of duties with the Porte, unless they were 
assured that the remedy for the specific grievance under which British 
commeree now labours should not im the meanwhile be delayed. That 

rievance is the exemption of Russian commerce from the Commutation 
dunes which press upon British commerce; and Her Majesty's Govern- 
ment heard with great satisfaction that the negotiations pending between 
Russia and the Porte promised to provide the remedy for that grievance 
by subjecting Russian commerce to the same amount of commutation 
duties as is stipulated in the Convention of 1838, and thereby placing the 
Russian and British trade, in respect to duties, on a footing of periect 
equality, 

Her Majesty's Government do not consider that it would form any 
serious impediment to the conclusion of a new and general arrangement on 
the basis suggested by the Cabinet of Vienna, if Russia should accede provi- 
sionally to an arrangement founded on the basis of the Convention of 1848 ; 
and if this preliminary measure could be expedited, the whole of the Powers, 
including Russia, would, Her Majesty’s Government conceive, be in a condi- 
tion to proceed with advantage to the consideration of the question, in what 
manner the general terms of the Convention of 1838 could be improved for all 
parties alike, and with a view especially to the relief of the Ottoman Empire 
from such disadvantages as it may be thought to suffer at present, in con- 
sequence of the particular form of the stipulations of the Convention of 
1838, relating to the Commutation Duties. 

But in order that any alterations in the existing system may be really 
beneficial, it will be requisite that sufficient time should be allowed for 
their consideration, and also that all parties should approach the subject 
onan equal footing. For this reason alone Her Majesty's Government 
would consider it advisable that, as a preliminary measure, the adoption 
of the scale of 1838 in the contemplated Convention between Russia and the 
Porte, should he promoted by all parties feeling an interest in the general 
question propounded by Austria, But Her Majesty's Government, in 
common with other Powers, and specifically with France, have the strongest 
reasons for desiring that the exceptional position which is now held by 
Russian trade should be changed. Your Excellency has been already 
authorized to warn the Porte that if the grievanee under which British 
trade now labours were not shortly remedied, Her Majesty's Government 
would have recourse to that specific remedy which the Treaty of ISH has 
provided in its First Article, Her Majesty's Government hoped that long 
hefore this, the conclusion of the projected Commercial Convention between 
Russia and the Porte would have established that equality between Russian 
and British trade, which ig all that they desire, In that expectation they 
have been disappointed; and they know not how long they may have to 
wait. unless the Porte will fairly consider how serions the question as it 
now stands is for England, and how serious will he the question for Turkey, 


3 


if Her Majesty's Government claim the benefit of the First Article of the 
Convention of 1838, 

That the Porte may not be taken unawares, | have to instruct your 
Excellency once more to warn the Porte of the consequences of delay. 
We have borne too long the disadvantages of the present state of things: 
but we wish to relieve ourselves from them in the manner least vexatious 
tothe Porte. We waitfor your Excellency’s reply to this instruction hefore 
we direct you to announce to the Porte that we require that British com- 
merce shall be placed in Turkey on precisely the same footing as Russian 
commerce, in the hope that your answer will convey the gratifying intelli- 
gence that an arrangement has been actually concluded between the 
Porte and Russia, which shall effect that object in the manner which we 
consider most conducive to the interests of the Porte. But if the result of 
your communication with the Turkish Ministers on this matter should 
lead to the conclusion that no such arrangement is likely to be speedily 
entered into, your Excellency will prepare the Porte for a demand on the 
part of Her Majesty's Government under the First Article of the Conven- 
tion, and you will intimate to it that all sums whieh from the date of that 
demand shall be levied on British trade on account of the commutation 
duties of 2 and 9 per cent. at any of the ports of ‘Turkey, will be required 
from the Turkish Government, and the payment of them, if necessary, 
enforced, 

| take this opportunity to remind your Excellency that 1am still in 
expectation of receiving from you the further information as to the eflect 
to be expected from a compliance with the wishes expressed by the Porte 
‘1 the memorandum inclosed in your despatch No. 227, of the 27th of 
October, 1842, for a modification of certain provisions of the Commercial 
Convention of 1838, which it was the object of my despatch No. 40 of the 
19th of March last, to elicit, 

[ shall transmit a copy of my present despatch to Her Majesty’s 
Representatives at Vienna, Paris, and St. Petersburgh, for communication 
to those Courts. 

Lam, &c.,

My Lord, Buyukdery, September 30, 1844, 

AGREEABLY to the instructions addressed to me by your Lordship 
on several occasions, | have continued to insist on the full execution of 
the Commercial Treaty with the Porte. Ihave pressed for satistactary 
assurances on the several points complained of, and | have neyer ceased to 
claim a full compensation for every British subject who has suffered from 
the arbitrary measures of the Turkish Ministry. 

he points more particularly in question, as your Lordship may 
remember, are these :— 

1. The re-establishment of certain kinds of monopoly. 2. The 
exclusion of British subjects from every branch of retail trade. 3, The 
imposition of highway tolls on British merchandize passing through the 
country. To these contraventions of the Commercial Treaty, might be 
added an attempt to levy certain internal duties on goods helonging to 
British merchants. But | have succeeded in resisting the latter preten- 
sion, and [ have already informed your Lordship that with respect to. the 
highway tolls, | obtained redress in the case brought forward by Her 
Majesty’s Consul in Albania. ’ 

Wie other individual cases of illegal exaction or of prohibition, 
whether to sell or to keep shop, are cither resisted altogether by the 
Porte, or so delayed iind embroiled as to remain virtually unredressed., 
‘Miese eases are nob numerous, but they are attended with great injury 


4 


to the merchants immediately concerned, and they no doubt operate gene- 
rally to the prejudice and discouragement of trade. They are amply 
stated in my official note to Rifaat Pasha, dated the 7th April, and trans- 
mitted to your Lordship, in copy, among the inclosures to my despatch 
No. 67 of the present year. Notwithstanding the assurances given by 
that Minister in his official answer received the Mth of April, ey have 
been constant subjects of discussion between the Porte and Her Majesty's 
Embassy ever since. ‘The Porte has now repeated its promise to settle 
the two principal claims, engaging to submit the demands of the claimants 
for loss and damages to the ae of an equitable commission, The time 
however which has clapsed since the first assurance was given, the 
chicaning spirit of the Financial Department, and the difficulty of obtain- 
ing a fair judgment, make it doubtlul whether the promised satisfaction 
will yet be obtained. But whether redress be obtained or not in these 
individual cases, the Porte still maintains on principle the pretensions in 
which they originated; and it is obvious that the interests both of Her 
Majesty's subjects and of Her Majesty's Embassy will continue to be 
exposed to much vexation and detriment, so long as the present uncer- 
tainty remains concerning those questions. The absence of any new 
speculations in the disputed articles of trade would itself go far to prove 
that commerce is checked on that side by the arbitrary proceedings of the 
Porte: and if the Porte be neither obliged to forego its pretensions, nor 
satisfied by some modification of the existing engagements, prolonged and 
irritating discussions will gradually kindle into feelings of resentment, 
and perhaps finally paralyze the influence of our friendly counsels at some 
decisive juncture, 

When we view the interests: at stake in connexion with those long- 
standing claims for which, under your Lordship’s special instructions, | 
have vainly sought redress by means of equitable inquiry and arbitration, 
the necessity of some determinate issue becomes still more apparent. In 
order to do justice to those instructions, and to secure a chance of suc- 
cess, it was indispensable at last to assume an attitude implying that the 
demands of Her Majeaty’s Government were not to be trifled with; and 
as no fresh arguments or facts are now brought forward in answer to 
them, although the old ground is formally maintained, T really do not see 
how the question can be left in its present stage without serious disad- 
vantage and discredit, 

For the particular terms of the Porte’s official reply on the cases 
involving commercial principles, | have the honour of referring your 
Lordship to the memorandum or note verbale addressed to me by Rifaat 
Pasha, and inclosed in translation with my despateh No, 212 of this date,

My Lord, Buyukdery, October 16, 1844. 
AMONG the commercial measures which at Sarim Effencdi’s request, 
and after much discussion with that Minister, | submitted to your Lane 
ship’s consideration in the years 1842-3, was the exhibition of ships’ 
manifests, as obligatory upon the captains of British vessels arriving in 
the ports of Turkey, and sarticularly in that of Constantinople. 1 did 
not at that time see any objection to the propore measure; and in my 
despatch No. 52 of March 18, 1843, Lremarked that the want of proper 
authority alone prevented me from acceding at once to the wishes of the 
Porte, In the course of this year the same measure has been frequently 
and earnestly pressed upon my acceptance by Rifaat Pasha, in connexion 
with other regulations brought forward by the Minister of Finance with 
a view to the suppression of smuggling. At the request of their Excel- 
lencies | obtained the opinions of our principal merchants upon the 
character and probable effect of the regulations in question, The unani- 
mous opinion of the merchants was unfavourable to most of the proposed 
measures, and particularly to the exhibition of the manifests. This re- 
sult of my appeal to them was not satisfactory to the Turkish Ministers ; 
and after repeated solicitations | was prevailed upon to call upon the 
merchants for a more deliberate consideration of the Porte’s proposals 
modified. Lapplied to them through Mr. Cartwright, who explained the 
points in question, and at my request invited them to make no objection 
without giving a reason and proposing a substitute, ‘Their answer to 
this requisition was similar in its eeneral purport to their previous opi- 
nion, though accompanied with counte r-proposals, in their judgment of a 
more efficient character than those of the Porte, ‘The Porte was not 
more satished with this result than with the former one ; and | have been 
given to understand that the difficulty thus made by our merchants has 
created much ill will towards the Embassy. ‘This eflect, if it be real, may 
have been aided by the facilities obtained from the French Ambassador, 
who was influenced by his commercial counsellors, whose trade is very 
limited, and by the desire, as he told me, to conciliate the Turkish Autho- 
rities after the affair of Moussul. Be that as it may, the Minister for 


7 


Foreign Affairs has recently returned to the charge, and sent me a memo- 
randum, detaching the question of the manifests from the other com- 
mercial questions and proposed regulations; and again most earnestly 
recommending it for a favourable consideration. 

On my part | have promised to submit the question separately to 
your Lordship’s superior judgment; and it will afford me much satisfac- 
tion to be channel of a favourable answer to the Porte, if those who are 
most versed in commercial questions in England should not see reason to 
attach a decisive importance to the remarks of the merchants here. 

Thinking it might be satisfactory to your Lordship to know the 
sentiments of Her Majesty's Sanaa Ganeral upon this matter, I applied

In our former report dated May 6, the objections to that measure

facilitate the professed object of the measure proposed, namely the 
putting a check upon smuggling ; and 3d, That it would give rise to 
delay in discharging cargoes, and be productive of misunderstanding’ 
between the merchants and the authorities. 

Adhering to this opinion, we may say that there being no precedent 
for this measure is rather a Turkish reason why it should not be acceded 
to, but it is one which the ‘Turks themselves very successfully put forward 
in opposition fo questions or demands of foreign authorities which they 
are not disposed to grant. If we did not apprehend disadvantages 
from it, or if we thought it would contribute to the very desirable 
object of checking smuggling, it would most assuredly have our cordial 
support. 

MWe stated the difficulties that were likely to arise from the uncertain 
information to be derived from the manifests, owing to a large proportion 
of the goods being consigned to order, to the uncertainty as to what 
portions might ultimately Es destined in transitu to other ports in Turkey

the trade was in danger of spreading the evil of contraband even wider 
than it now exists. 

It ig well known that British trade with ‘Turkey is now almost 
exclusively carried on by foreigners, in consequence of the great 
advantages enjoyed by Russians over British merchants in regard to 
duties: and such being the case it can hardly be doubted that were the 
proposed measure acceded to, manifests would soon be prepared in such a 
way as to answer all the purposes of contraband, which it is supposed 
that measure would check. In fact the more we consider the 

weation the more we are convinced that the production of mani- 
ests to the custom-house would not realise the objects in view. 

We stated in our former report that the ‘Turkish employés for the 
prevention ofsmuggling were not recompensed in such a way as to make 
them faithful to their duties, and this consideration strengthens our ob- 
jections to the placing more control than is now exereised, in the hands of 
the custom-house authorities, as it would not in our opinion tend to pre- 
vent smuggling, whilst on the other hand we feel convinced it would be a 
great. ne to the legitimate trade.

_ The first step we conceive would be necessary is—that all the foreign 

Missions should agree upon the question of fine to be levied in cases of 
clearly proved acts of smuggling or contraband, without which no regu-

: Such a penalty is by no means a sufficient punishment for an offence

Mr. Sandison's Protest. 


Brussa, May 18, 1845. 


THE undersigned, Her Britannic Majesty's Consul, has been officially 
informed by the Dsuaner Mahomet Ali Aga, of this city, that he levies 
24 per cent, of internal duty on British manufactures when made up into 
dresses for sale in the public bazaars here, and further 12 per cent, customs 
with fees, 134 per cent. nearly on the same articles at their exit from the 
town for internal consumption elsewhere, the ground assipned heing 
that the goods have changed their primitive form. . 

Under the application of this vague term, susceptible of every sort of 
extension, any imported merchandize might in the first stage of its distri- 
bution to retailers or consumers be subjected to taxation without limit. 

When British cotton yarns are wrought up into local manufactures, 
it is not questioned that they lose their distinctive original character; nor 
is it necessary to touch here on what different, species of process may 
operate the like conversion in regard to other articles, so that the product 
may come properly under the designation of Turkish manufactures, 

It is sufficient to refer to the Commercial Convention of 1838 between 
Great Britain and Turkey, based on general definite principles, without 
detailing the special contingencies incident to its operation; and it esta- 
blishes certain fixed duties on commodities in lieu of all other whatsoever, 
which are 5 per cent. customs in full on British imported merchandize, 

The superaddition of internal duties, and to the extent of 16 per cent. 
on the goods when merely put into a suitable form for use, without any 
change in the substance or appearance of the stuffs em loyed, would 
entirely destroy the validity of the First Additional Article of the Treaty, if 
consistent with its tenor or spirit; and whether the articles are exhibited 
ready made in the public shops, or the merchandize is shaped into dresses 
or household furnishings of all sorts in any other way convenient for the 
owners, the process of cutting and stitching is the same, and the principle 
of taxation adopted alike applicable. 

The interests and immunities of British commerce are thus seriously 
compromised, with the care and protection of which the undersigned is 
charged in this district, and its import trade under this recent innovation 
is visibly decayed. , 

The undersigned therefore formally protests Mahomet Ali Aga, the 
Douanier, and whomsoever else it may concern, on account of the afore- 
said exactions, inasmuch as opposed pie spree terms or true construc- 
tion of the Commercial Convention of Balta Liman, | 

(Signed) D. SANDISON, 
Her Britannic Majesty’s Consul.

Sir, British Consulate, Bagdad, February 3, 1846. 

IN submitting for your Excellency’s perusal copy of a letter that J 
have received from Messrs. Alexander Hector and Co., merchants of Bag- 
dad, drawing attention to certain matters which operate unfavourably 
on the prosecution of British trade in this part of the Turkish Empire, | 
do myself the honour to annex @ few observations. 

Messrs. Hector and Co, complain in the first place, that the Con- 
stantinople prices, according to which certain fixed duties are established 
in the English Tariff, vary 80 essentially from those of the provincial mar- 
kets, that if the rates of the said ‘Tariff be adopted as the standard of 
liability, the interior and export duties on some of the staple articles of 
Mesopotamia produce will be raised from 12 to nearly 25 per cent. 
Hitherto it appears that the urkish authorities have been content to 
levy an ad valorem duty, and the evil therefore has not been practically 
felt of the disproportioned rates adopted in the Tariff, but the merchants 
are not satisfied that this moderation will be continued, unless the principle 
of an ad valorem duty be specifically recognised. In the Fourth Article 
of the Convention of Balta Liman, [ perceive that the internal duties, 
commuted to a fixed rate of 9 per cent,, are positively stated as an ad 
valorem liability—a provision of which the merehants might possibly avail 
themselves in defining the sum leviable on that account, if in regard to 
any particular article of produce, the rates established in the Tariff should 
be notoriously inapplicable ; but Leannot find that any such latitude 1s 
allowed in arranging for the export duty of 3 per cent., although the fre- 
quent substitution in the ‘Tariff of 3 per cent. ad valorem for a fixed duty, 
and the application of the same rate to all non-enumerated goods, dis- 
tinetly mark the principle upon which the amount is calculated ; and 
although the preamble to the Russian Tariff particularly mentions that 
in all cases of dispute regarding value, the duty of 3 a cent. shall be 
paid in kind. Tn the event of a revision of the ‘Tariff, the uncertainty of 
which the merchants now complain might at any rate he obviated by 
making it optional with the exporter to pay the fixed duty in money, or 
one-eighth, one-third of the actual produce. 

A more important question appears to me however to refer to the 
place where the ad valorem duty may be leviable. The 4th Article of 
the Convention of Balta Liman provides for the exaction of the entire 
duty at the place of shipment, and in the 7th Article it is also declared 
that these places of shipment shall be subsequently fixed by commis- 
signers. Now in this part of the Turkish Empire the above provision 
has been wholly inoperative ; and to such an accident is it alone to be 
attributed that the export of BANS produce has been at all practicable 


46 


for British merchants. The places of shipment for the wool, galls, &c., 
exported from this pashalic for the European markets, are Samsoon, 
Alexandretta, or Beyrout, ports which are so distant that the goods are 
doubled in value before arriving there, by the enormous expence of land 
carriage ; and if the duty of 12 per cent. accordingly be levied ad valorem 
at these places, instead of being calculated on the prime cost at the place 
of production, the exportation will cease to be remunerative. This re- 
mark must 1 imagine apply to all distant provices of the Turkish Empire. 
Produce is cheap in those places on aceount of the expense of land car- 
riage to the market-port of shipment for Europe, but on arriving at the 
port the article is rarely able to compete with produce obtained in the 
vicinity; and if therefore it be the wish of the Porte to admit those pro- 
vinces to a participation in the supply of foreign markets, she must accord 
to them the privilege of paying duty on the spot, rather than at the port 
of shipment. 

In connexion with this subject [have drawn up an abstract state- 
ment of the export and import trade of the Bagdad Pashalic during the 
past year, which your Excellency may perhaps consider to be of some 
interest. It is particularly worthy of remark that the balance of trade 
whether with Europe, through Syria and Asia Minor, with India and 
Rahrein, or with Persia direct, is equally against Bagdad, The quantity 
of specie annually exported to provide for the imports from these quarters 
amounts to at least 40 lacs of kerans, or about 200,000/, The remit- 
tances of the Government on account of surplus revenue should increase 
the withdrawal of money from the country by about 50,000/,, and as a set 
off against this extraordinary drain of 250,000/. per annum, there is no 
perceptible return but the experditure of the Persian pilgrims, which, 
reckoning’ the total number of visitors at 50,000, and the receipts at 50 
kerans per head, will only account for a moiety of the sum withdrawn. 

Money probably finds its way into the country in small sums from 
other quarters; but unquestionably the circulating medium is yearly 
upinihige: and unless native produce be encouraged so as to offer some 
means of marketable exchange, the trade of the province must inevitably 
sive way before the exhaustion of specie. It is in this view then that | 
think your Excellency will agree in its being for the interests of the Porte 
to extend every possible Aral eencs to parties engaged in the export of 
wool and galls for the European markets. Under favourable circum- 
stances [ have reason to believe that the export of wool might be in- 
ereased tenfold, but to create this activity it will be indispensable that 
the article shall reach the ports of shipment at a less cost than it can be 
procured in the immediate vicinity of those places; and if therefore the 
present exorbitant export duty must be retained, | should hope at any 
rate that the Turkish authorities might be induced to sanction its ’pay- 
ment at the place of production, before the additional expence may be 
incurred of a thousand miles of carriage to the sea. 

With regard to the other complaints which are brought forward by 
Messrs. Hector and Co,, | may safely leave them, I think, to your Excel- 
lency's Pra judgment, ‘The duty and realization, intended no doubt 
originally as a mere remunerative fee to the party engaged in so trouble- 
some a business, has certainly grown into an abuse; but as it is specially 
provided for in Article 73rd of the Capitulations and as it has been now 
in practice for above 200 years, I imagine there may be some difficulty im 
procuring its abolition or its transfer to the native debtor. The question 
also of sequestrating household property in order to satisfy the demand of 
a Christian creditor, is so repugnant to the spirit of Mahomedan law and 
usage, that I should hardly venture to hope it could be favourably enter- 
tained by the Ministers of the Sublime Porte,

My Lord, Constantinople, March 18, 1846. 

I HAVE the honour to place under your Lordship’s immediate notice 
a correspondence, of which copies are herewith inclosed, between Her 
Majesty’s Consul-General and myself, respecting a revision of our Tariff 
with the Porte, and the operation of the Turkish stamp duty. 

I have but little to remark in addition to what | addressed to the 
merchants through Mr, Cumberbatch. 

My impression is that the merchants are correct in looking upon the 
present Tariff as raising the duties to an unfair proportion on some 
important articles; and [ shall be happy to receive at an early period 
your Lordship's authority for demanding a revision of that agreement. 

The time for giving notice began on the 13th instant, and terminates 
on the 13th of September next. 

With respect to the stamp duty | have no doubt that it operate more 
unfavourably than would seem at first sight on the interests of trade, and 
that itis liable to be continually extended and even to serve as a prece- 
dent for the imposition of other burthens operating indirectly on com- 
meree, But it is difficult to imagine that the Porte will entirely abandon 
its right of internal taxation, until some clearer understanding be estab- 
lished as to the limitations prescribed by the Convention of Balta Liman, 
According to the terms of that Treaty the duties imposed upon British 
owned merchandize are not to execed 9 per cent., and 2 per cent. in lieu of 
all internal duties; and at the same time the Porte has expressly reserved 
the rights of its internal administration, The operation of the Treaty is 
thus placed in a vicious circle, and frequent differences and complaints 
must arise from time to time so long as the true line of demarcation 
hetween the conflicting pretensions remains undefined and unknown.
