# Chapter 11 (Twelve O'Clock Precisely, 1846–1847): claims cut or corrected

Copied verbatim from the "Corrections to the original" section of book/11-narrative.md (local narrative rewrite, 47961 bytes). It records what in the original chapter the sources contradicted or could not support, and what was done about it. Chapter text was not changed.

## Corrections to the original

- The Providence sentence is from the letter of 9 October 1846, but the original adds words Trevelyan did not write. The letter says "the deep and inveterate root of social evil remains" and "this being altogether beyond the power of man." It does not say "the overpopulation of Ireland." The transcriptions read "as unexpected and unthought of," not "as unexpected and as unthought of."
- The original says Monteagle "asked what more the government intended to do." His letters of 1 October have not been published; Trevelyan's reply shows only that Monteagle wrote "letters dated 1st instant." The chapter does not characterise them.
- The original calls Trevelyan's letter "policy" and says the Gregory clause put it "into public statute, word for word in effect." The letter is a private letter to a former Chancellor of the Exchequer. The clause was moved by William Gregory, the Member for Dublin, in committee on 29 March 1847 and carried 117 to 7, with the Government's support. It became law in the Act of 8 June 1847, 10 & 11 Vict. c. 31, to take effect from 1 November 1847. The original's "eight months before Gregory drafted it" is wrong.
- The original says the clause required a family to "surrender the land, in writing, to the landlord." The clause as moved requires the applicant to have "bonâ fide, and without collusion, absolutely parted with and surrendered" his right to land over a quarter of a statute acre. It says nothing of writing or of the landlord.
- The original calls the writer of the letter to Wellington "a Cork magistrate named Nicholas Cummins." He signed himself "N. M. Cummins, J.P., Ann Mount, Cork." The Treasury's Cork market correspondent, Nicholas Cummins of N. and J. Cummins, Anderson's Quay, is a different man, who called the writer "a near relative of my own." The chapter keeps them apart.
- The original says Cummins "never claimed to be arguing with Treasury policy." His letter accuses those who "have the power to send relief, and do not," and asks the Duke to "break the frigid and flimsy chain of official etiquette."
- The original attributes a character sketch of Trevelyan to Cecil Woodham-Smith. It is a modern historian's judgment and is removed.
- The crowbar brigades, the demolitions "the same day a family walked out," and the landlords' rate liability on holdings under £4 are not supported by any source used for this chapter, and are removed.
- The original's own source list cites web pages (Legal Legacy, The Tree Sentinels, Gript, Irish Memorial, Encyclopedia.com) and an uploaded "deep-research synthesis." All are replaced by the printed parliamentary papers, Hansard, the NLI manuscripts and the newspaper of the day.
