# Chapter 13 (Banana Skins at the Metal Bridge, 1913–1915): claims cut or corrected

Copied verbatim from the "Corrections to the original" section of book/13-narrative.md (local narrative rewrite, 55030 bytes). It records what in the original chapter the sources contradicted or could not support, and what was done about it. Chapter text was not changed.

## Corrections to the original

- The original says Mrs. Duffy, James Brennan and Patrick Quinn were "killed outright" and that "a fourth died later of bayonet wounds." Mrs. Duffy and Quinn were brought in dead. Brennan was brought to Jervis Street alive at about 7.30 with a bullet wound and bayonet wounds to the right thigh and died there about 8.30. The fourth death was Sylvester Pidgeon, who died on 28 September 1914 of a bullet wound to the leg, not a bayonet wound.
- The original says "More than thirty" were wounded. The Royal Commission found at least thirty-eight injured, fifteen detained in hospital. Birrell told the House on 27 July three killed and thirty-two injured, and thirty-one rounds fired; the Commission found twenty-nine shots from twenty-one men. Both counts are given with their sources.
- The original has the Assistant Commissioner declare an illegal assembly. Hobson recalled Harrel calling them "an illegal body, illegally importing arms." The Commission accepted that Harrel had the notion of an unlawful assembly in his mind, but found that "his real and true object was the seizure of the contraband rifles," a seizure only Customs officers or men employed against smuggling could lawfully make, and that there was "no case warranting military intervention." The chapter gives the Commission's finding.
- The original says the King's Own Scottish Borderers "failed to catch them at Clontarf." The Borderers and police met the column on the Malahide Road, seized about nineteen rifles in a struggle with bayonets and clubbed rifles, and the rest of the column dispersed across the fields.
- The original gives "roughly 900 secondhand Mauser rifles left over from the Franco-Prussian War" and 29,000 rounds. Figgis bought 1,500 rifles and 45,000 rounds; Hobson says 900 rifles and 26,000 rounds went to Howth and the rest to Kilcoole. No primary source used here traces the rifles to the war of 1870, and the phrase is removed.
- The original says the unloading took twenty minutes. Hobson says about half an hour. The chapter gives no figure.
- The original gives Larne as "roughly 25,000 rifles and between three and five million rounds." Crawford's account, through McNeill, gives 20,000 modern rifles and 10,000 Italian rifles and upwards of 3,000,000 rounds. The chapter gives Crawford's figures. The claim that Larne was "the first large-scale military use of the motor car" has no primary source and is removed.
- The original quotes Paget saying the country "would be in a blaze." The only primary source found is Seely in the Commons, who said Paget denied the words and said "there might be a blaze, certainly a blaze in the Press." Gough's "Irish by blood and upbringing" and the figure of "roughly sixty officers" were not found in a primary source and are removed. The original says the signers of the assurance resigned; on 25 March Asquith refused Seely's resignation, and the chapter goes no further than that day.
- The original sets the Asgard rendezvous "off Belgium." Figgis places it at a lightship at the mouth of the Scheldt on 12 July. He calls the two fishermen "Donegal fishermen"; the original's "Gola" is not in any source used here.
- The original's closing paragraph states its own conclusion about a double standard. The Commission found no evidence that the Government treated Ulster and Dublin differently and laid the difference on Harrel alone. The chapter gives that finding, Birrell's and Redmond's words, the constables' reason for refusing and the record of Larne, and does not state a conclusion.
- Ages and names differ between sources. The Commission gives Brennan as 18, Quinn as 46 and Mrs. Duffy as 50; the hospital list gives Brennan 20 and Quinn 30. The chapter follows the Commission. The hospital list calls Pidgeon "Sebastian"; the Capuchin flier calls him Sylvester. Birrell in the Commons and Crewe in the Lords gave the Borderers sent out as 160 in all; the Commission gives fifty across the road with more behind.
- Figgis and Hobson say some Volunteers fired revolvers on the Malahide Road. The Commission found the two shots were fired "not by the Volunteers, but by some bystanders unknown." The chapter reports two shots and does not name who fired them.
- The original's sources (Irish News, History Today, Irish Times, Century Ireland, An Phoblacht, Gript) are replaced by the Royal Commission's report and evidence, Hansard, McNeill's transcription of Crawford, Figgis, and the Bureau of Military History.
