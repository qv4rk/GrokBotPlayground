# Chapter 08 (A Shilling a Turnip, 1879–1880): claims cut or corrected

Copied verbatim from the "Corrections to the original" section of book/08-narrative.md (local narrative rewrite, 53203 bytes). It records what in the original chapter the sources contradicted or could not support, and what was done about it. Chapter text was not changed.

## Corrections to the original

- The original's "£10,000" for the cost of the expedition and "roughly three hundred and fifty pounds" for the crop are not found together in any primary source used here. Becker reckoned the root crops in the ground in October at "at least five hundred pounds." Davitt gave about £350 as Boycott's own estimate of what was lifted and £3,500 as the cost by calculations of the time. Parnell, in his letter to America, said every pound of potatoes and every turnip had cost the Government a shilling. The figure of £10,000 and the ratio of "thirty times" are removed.
- The original says "nearly nine hundred soldiers and police." The Annual Register gives about 900 soldiers at Ballinrobe and 7,000 police and military engaged in Mayo; Becker counted some 750 military and nearly two hundred constabulary at Ballinrobe on 10 November.
- The original says "fifty volunteers." Becker counted sixty-three officers and men of the Ulster contingent at Claremorris; Boycott had asked for fifty.
- The original says the volunteers arrived at Claremorris on 12 November (in its source note). They arrived on Thursday, 11 November, and marched to Lough Mask House on Friday, 12 November.
- The original credits the expedition to Captain Somerset Maxwell alone. Becker names Somerset Maxwell for Cavan and Mr. Goddard, a solicitor, for Monaghan, and Mr. Manning's letters in the Dublin Daily Express as the movement's origin.
- The original gives Boycott "roughly 1,500 acres worked by about 120 tenant families," "five hundred pounds a year plus ten percent," and a twenty-five per cent abatement asked against ten per cent offered. None of these was found in a primary source used here; they are removed. Redpath says the tenants asked "a moderate reduction" and were refused.
- The original says the women drove the process-server off "with stones, mud, and manure" as settled fact. That account is Redpath's, given to an American interviewer in 1881 by a Land League partisan, and is attributed to him; Boycott's letter speaks of "a howling mob of people." Redpath names one woman, Mrs. Fitzmaurice.
- The original says that on 23 September "roughly a hundred people" told Boycott's employees to stop work "and every one of them did." This is Boycott's own account in his letter and is given as his.
- The original says "No one laid a hand on him" and calls the campaign "entirely nonviolent." The record shows a process-server knocked down and stoned (Redpath), a twelve-year-old post-boy struck (Boycott), a murder threat to a blacksmith (Boycott) and a car-driver "cruelly beaten" (Becker). No source records Boycott himself being touched.
- The original says the Land League was founded "specifically to fight for fair rent, fixity of tenure, and free sale, the 'Three Fs.'" The resolution of 21 October 1879 gave its objects as the reduction of rack-rents and the ownership of the soil by the occupiers.
- The original says the Times "had already begun using his surname as a common verb." Redpath, who claimed a share in it, said the word was coined by Father John O'Malley at the Neale and first written by himself; the Annual Register says the system was "henceforward known as 'Boycotting.'" The claim about the Times is removed.
- The original quotes a placard of Father O'Malley addressed to the "Men of Mayo," praising them for having "manfully" upheld the cause. No primary text of it was found; the quotation is removed. The Annual Register records anonymous proclamations calling on the men of Mayo to resent the invasion, and Davitt a League manifesto telling the people to leave the expedition alone.
- The original says Boycott left "that December, quietly, in an army ambulance wagon escorted by a troop of the 19th Hussars." The Annual Register says he left with the expedition, about 26 November; Davitt says 1 December. No primary source used here mentions the ambulance; it is removed.
- The original says the returning column met roads that were "empty" and that "a Belfast newspaper reporter called the silence itself remarkable." The Annual Register records "ostentatious indifference" and children's "ironical laughter" at Ballinrobe; the Belfast reporter was not found and is removed.
- The original's sources (the Dictionary of Irish Biography, History Ireland, the Anglo-Celt, Mayo County Council and three secondary monographs) are replaced by the Freeman's Journal, MacPhilpin's depositions, the Mansion House report, the Annual Register for 1880, Boycott's letter to the Times, Redpath, Becker and Davitt.
